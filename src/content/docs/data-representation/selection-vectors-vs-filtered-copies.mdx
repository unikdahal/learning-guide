A filter decides, for each row of a batch, whether it continues. The decision is cheap: a comparison per row,
producing one bit. What happens next is the real design question. The engine can copy the passing rows of
every column into a new, smaller batch, or it can keep the original batch and pass along a description of
which rows survived: a bitmap, or a list of row indices called a **selection vector**. Each choice moves cost
somewhere else, and which is best depends on how many rows pass and what comes next.

## The question

How can a filter's result be represented, and what does each representation cost the operators that follow?

## Three representations

- **A bitmap**, one bit per input row. It's what the comparison produces anyway, so it costs nothing extra to
  make. Every later operator still visits every row and checks or applies its bit.
- **A selection vector**, the indices of the passing rows, typically 32-bit. It costs time to build from the
  bitmap and space in proportion to the rows that pass, and later operators touch only those rows, through the
  indices.
- **A filtered copy**: new arrays holding only the passing values of every column. It costs one copy of every
  column of every passing row. Afterwards, everything downstream works on dense arrays with simple loops.

## Measured: one filter, several strategies

The lab builds a batch of 10 million rows with three `Int64` columns and one string column, and random masks
that keep 0.1% to 90% of rows. It measures making a filtered copy of the whole batch with arrow-rs's
`filter_record_batch`, building a selection vector, and computing `sum(a)` over the selected rows three ways:
filter column a and sum it, gather a through the selection vector, or apply the bitmap directly to all of a.

    90%    304.91 ms    33.44 ms    51.51 ms     59.92 ms     15.47 ms     17.11 ms

<Aside type="caution" title="Run-to-run variation">
The middle columns moved by up to about two times between runs; one earlier run had `idx+sum a` at 9.6 ms for
50%, another at 17.7. The shapes, and the crossover between 10% and 50%, held in every run.

**At low selectivity, touch only what passed.** With 0.1% of rows passing, gathering 10,000 values through a
selection vector took 0.06 ms, while the bitmap-driven sum still read all 80 MB of column a: 270 times slower.
Building the vector cost more than using it, but even together they were 46 times cheaper.

**At high selectivity, skip the indirection.** The bitmap sum costs the same at every selectivity, about 16 to
18 ms, because it always reads the whole column and does the same branch-free work per value. Somewhere between
10% and 50% of rows passing, it overtakes both alternatives, which pay per selected row and then some.

**Copies cost per column.** Copying all four columns took up to 305 ms, against 60 ms for copying column a alone.
A filtered copy pays for every column whether or not anything downstream reads it. The string column is the
most expensive part: its bytes must be copied too, and its offsets rebuilt.

**Large copies can pay the operating system as well.** At 90%, the copy holds 369 MB, and that one copy took
81,320 page faults; at 50% and below it took none, reusing memory already mapped. At the 2.2 µs per fault
measured in the [perf counters article](../../hardware/measuring-hardware-with-perf-counters/), that is roughly
180 ms of the 305: likely most of the jump from 50% to 90%. Why the allocator returned that memory at one size
and not another wasn't investigated.

## What engines do

Engines don't pick one representation for everything.

**arrow-rs's filter kernel** produces copies but chooses how: when more than 80% of rows pass it copies runs of
consecutive passing rows as slices, and otherwise it iterates the indices of set bits. DataFusion builds on it,
so its filters materialize a new batch, and it later merges small filtered batches into larger ones so
downstream operators don't process many tiny batches.

**Vectorized engines in the Vectorwise tradition**, and DuckDB and Velox today, carry selection vectors between
operators instead. A column can be wrapped with a selection vector, in effect a
[dictionary array](../dictionary-and-run-end-encoded-arrays/) whose keys are row indices into the unfiltered
column, so a filter followed by another filter or an aggregation never copies the data at all.

**Late materialization** generalizes the idea: carry row positions through filters and joins, and copy values
of the other columns only for the rows that reach the end. It is one of the techniques Abadi, Madden and Hachem
found responsible for much of column stores' advantage ([row vs column layout](../row-vs-column-layout/)).

## Costs and trade-offs

**Bitmaps cost per input row.** Free to produce and branch-free to apply, they are the best choice when most rows
pass, and wasteful when few do.

**Selection vectors cost per passing row, plus indirection.** Ideal for selective filters, they make every later
access a gather, and every operator must understand them.

**Copies cost per passing row per column, once.** They are expensive with many or wide columns and simple for
everything downstream: kernels see plain dense arrays and nothing else.

**The best choice can change batch by batch.** The same filter may pass 1% of one batch and 90% of the next;
engines that decide per batch, as arrow-rs's filter does between slices and indices, track that.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>A filter passes 0.5% of rows and the next operator sums one column. Which representation fits, and why?</summary>

A selection vector, or a copy of just that column. Both touch only the passing rows; a bitmap would make the sum
read the whole column to use half a percent of it.
</details>

<details>
<summary>A filter passes 95% of a 30-column batch, and the next operator reads two columns. Why is a full copy a poor choice?</summary>

It would copy 95% of all 30 columns to save reading 5% of two. Keeping the bitmap, or copying only the two columns
used, does far less work.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why was the bitmap-driven sum about the same speed at every selectivity?</summary>

It reads every value of the column and combines each with its bit without branching, so the work doesn't depend on
how many bits are set.
</details>
