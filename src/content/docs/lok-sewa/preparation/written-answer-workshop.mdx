**Writing heuristics (learning aids, not an official marking scheme):**

1. **Follow the marks split.** "Define X. Explain Y. (2 + 8)" gets two clearly separated parts. Put the definition first and keep it short.
2. **About one distinct point per mark** in theory answers. A 10-mark answer is roughly 6–8 points plus a diagram, or a full worked numerical. It is not three pages of prose.
3. **Draw whenever a diagram exists.** Protocol stacks, process state diagrams, DFDs, ER diagrams, trees and timing charts are quick to mark and hard to fake.
4. **Comparisons go in a table** with 5–7 rows for 5 marks. For "compare and explain", add one sentence per row below it.
5. **Numericals show every step**: given, formula, substitution, result with units, one-line interpretation. With no calculator, keep the arithmetic exact (powers of two, fractions).
6. **Use standard terms** (ACID, Coffman conditions, CIDR, FIRST/FOLLOW) so the examiner can spot the keywords at a glance.

### Generic 10-mark rubric (learning aid)

| Criterion | Share | What full marks looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy of core content | 40% | every point correct; no confusion of terms |
| Coverage | 25% | all parts of the question answered in proportion to their marks |
| Diagram / worked example | 20% | labelled, correct, referred to in the text |
| Structure and clarity | 10% | headings per part, numbered points, table for comparisons |
| Context / judgement | 5% | a sensible example, limitation or recommendation |

## 2. Official sample: tree from preorder and postorder

**Source: Official sample** (CO syllabus, Paper II, S2). *Preorder: u1 u2 u3 u4 u10 u8 u5 u9 u6 u11 u7. Postorder: u4 u10 u3 u8 u2 u9 u11 u7 u6 u5 u1. Obtain the binary tree.*

**Key fact to state first:** preorder and postorder together do **not** fix a binary tree in general. If a node has only one child, you can't tell whether it is the left or the right child. They do fix a **full** binary tree, where every node has 0 or 2 children. So build assuming the tree is full, then check that the assumption holds.

**Method (recursive):**
1. The root is the first node of the preorder and the last of the postorder: **u1**.
2. The next preorder node, **u2**, is the root of the left subtree. In the postorder, everything up to and including u2 (`u4 u10 u3 u8 u2`) is the left subtree, and the rest before u1 (`u9 u11 u7 u6 u5`) is the right subtree.
3. Recurse:
   - Left (pre `u2 u3 u4 u10 u8`, post `u4 u10 u3 u8 u2`): root u2. Its left child is u3, whose postorder block is `u4 u10 u3`, so u3 has children u4 and u10. u2's right child is u8.
   - Right (pre `u5 u9 u6 u11 u7`, post `u9 u11 u7 u6 u5`): root u5. Its left child is u9 (a leaf); its right child is u6, with children u11 and u7.

## 3. Official sample: merge two heaps

**Source: Official sample** (CO syllabus, Paper II, S1). The PDF draws **H1 "a max heap"**: 9 with children 4 and 6, and 4 with children 3 and 5. It draws **H2 "a min heap"**: 1 with children 19 and 80, 19 with children 9 and 9, and 80 with child 96.

**Read the drawings critically.** Neither is a valid heap as printed. In H1, 5 is a child of 4, which breaks the max-heap property. In H2, 9 is a child of 19, which breaks the min-heap property. Say so in one line, then state your assumption. This makes your reasoning and assumptions checkable; the official marking scheme is not available.

> *Assumption: the heaps are taken as the level-order arrays H1 = [9, 4, 6, 3, 5] and H2 = [1, 19, 80, 9, 9, 96], and the result is to be a **max heap**.*

**Method: concatenate, then build bottom-up (Floyd's heapify), O(n + m).** Put all 11 keys in one array and sift down every internal node from index ⌊11/2⌋ − 1 = 4 down to 0:

| Step | Sift node (value) | Array after the step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| start | — | 9 4 6 3 5 1 19 80 9 9 96 |
| i = 4 | 5 | 9 4 6 3 **96** 1 19 80 9 9 **5** |
| i = 3 | 3 | 9 4 6 **80** 96 1 19 **3** 9 9 5 |
| i = 2 | 6 | 9 4 **19** 80 96 1 **6** 3 9 9 5 |
| i = 1 | 4 | 9 **96** 19 80 **9** 1 6 3 9 **4** 5 |
| i = 0 | 9 | **96 80** 19 **9** 9 1 6 3 9 4 5 |

**Also worth one line each:**
- For an already valid H1, merging by **repeated insertion** costs O(m log(n + m)). The printed H1 is invalid, so heapify it first if using that method. Floyd's build is O(n + m), so it is the better answer.
- If the question means "merge into a **min** heap", the same build with the comparison reversed gives [1, 3, 6, 4, 5, 9, 19, 80, 9, 9, 96].
- Binary heaps have no fast merge. **Leftist, binomial or Fibonacci heaps** merge in O(log n) or O(1), which is a good closing remark.

## 4. Official sample: relational algebra and SQL

**Source: Official sample** (CO syllabus, Paper II, S5; PSC prints a solution). Schema: `Suppliers(sid, sname, address)`, `Parts(pid, pname, color)`, `Catalog(sid, pid, cost)`. *Find the names of suppliers who supply some red part.*

```text
Relational algebra (PSC's form):
  π sname ( π sid ( π pid ( σ color='red' (Parts) ) ⋈ Catalog ) ⋈ Suppliers )

Equivalent, with the selection pushed down and attributes named:
  RedParts ← π pid ( σ color='red' (Parts) )
  RedSids  ← π sid ( RedParts ⋈ Catalog )
  Answer   ← π sname ( RedSids ⋈ Suppliers )
```

```sql
-- PSC's printed SQL
SELECT S.sname
FROM   Suppliers S, Parts P, Catalog C
WHERE  P.color = 'red' AND C.pid = P.pid AND C.sid = S.sid;

-- Better: explicit joins and DISTINCT
SELECT DISTINCT S.sname
FROM   Suppliers S
JOIN   Catalog  C ON C.sid = S.sid
JOIN   Parts    P ON P.pid = C.pid
WHERE  P.color = 'red';
```

**The point that lifts this answer above the printed one:** relational algebra works on **sets**, but SQL returns a **multiset** (bag). A supplier who supplies two red parts appears **twice** in PSC's SQL output. In a test with supplier "Acme" supplying two red parts, the printed query returned Acme, Acme, Chet; the `DISTINCT` version returned Acme, Chet. Add `DISTINCT` and say why in one line. Also mention that two suppliers with the same name would merge under `DISTINCT sname`, so include `S.sid` as well if the requested result must distinguish supplier identities. For a names-only set projection, `DISTINCT S.sname` matches that projection.

**Variants to practise** (Original practice): suppliers of red **or** green parts (∪, or `IN ('red','green')`); suppliers of red **and** green parts (∩, or `GROUP BY … HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT color) = 2`); suppliers who supply **every** part (÷, or `NOT EXISTS … NOT EXISTS`); the cheapest supplier of each part (`GROUP BY pid` with `MIN(cost)` joined back). All four come up again in [DBMS lesson 2](/learning-guide/nec/dbms/02-relational-sql/).

## 6. Transcribed CE 2078: subnetting with host .177

**Source: Transcription** (CE 2078 Q2, 10 marks). *200.168.10.0/24 is subnetted into 7 subnetworks; host 200.168.10.177. Find the subnet mask, network address, broadcast address, usable host range, and the subnet the host is in.*

1. **Bits to borrow:** 2ⁿ ≥ 7 gives n = 3, so 8 subnets. New prefix /24 + 3 = **/27**.
2. **Mask:** 11111111.11111111.11111111.**111**00000 = **255.255.255.224**. Block size 256 − 224 = **32**, so 32 addresses and **30 usable hosts** per subnet.
3. **Subnets:** .0, .32, .64, .96, .128, .160, .192, .224.
4. **Locate .177:** 177 ÷ 32 = 5 remainder 17, so the block starts at 5 × 32 = **160**. In binary, 177 = 101**1 0001**: AND with 224 = 1110 0000 gives 1010 0000 = 160.

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Subnet mask | 255.255.255.224 (/27) |
| Network address | 200.168.10.160 |
| Broadcast address | 200.168.10.191 |
| Usable host range | 200.168.10.161 – 200.168.10.190 (30 hosts) |
| Host lies in | the **6th subnet** (subnet number 5 when counting from 0) |

## 7. Short answers: the 5-mark shape

**Source: Transcription** (CE 2078 Q4, 2 + 3). *What is deadlock? What are the necessary conditions for deadlock to occur?* About 8 minutes and half a page:

> **Deadlock** is a state in which each process in a set waits for a resource held by another process in the same set, so none of them can ever proceed. *(2)*
>
> **Necessary conditions (Coffman, 1971)**, which must all hold at once: *(3)*
> 1. **Mutual exclusion**: at least one resource is non-shareable.
> 2. **Hold and wait**: a process holds one resource while waiting for another.
> 3. **No pre-emption**: a resource is released only voluntarily by its holder.
> 4. **Circular wait**: there is a cycle P₀ → P₁ → … → Pₙ → P₀ of processes, each waiting for a resource held by the next.
>
> *Small resource-allocation graph:* P1 holds R1 and requests R2; P2 holds R2 and requests R1, which forms a cycle. Prevention breaks any one of the four conditions.

That is all a 5-mark answer needs. Don't add avoidance and Banker's algorithm unless asked; that belongs in the [10-mark version](/learning-guide/nec/os/04-deadlock/).

## 8. Technical writing: the 10-mark Section D answer

**Official wording (CE 16.1):** *"This section may ask the examinee to write technical proposal on given specification, essay, views/critics etc. The topic must be related to computer technology … and examinee should be encouraged to put his/her own views and reasoning rather than facts/information."*

So the examiner wants **your reasoning**, organised. The transcribed papers show both forms: a **concept note** (2078: *automate attendance, payroll, registration*) and a **views essay** (2079: *ICT against the direct and indirect challenges of COVID-19*).

### Template A: concept note or proposal (about 16 minutes, 1.5–2 pages)

1. **Title** and one-line purpose.
2. **Background / problem** (3–4 lines): what goes wrong today, with numbers if you can estimate them honestly ("about 40 staff, paper attendance register, payroll prepared manually in a spreadsheet each month").
3. **Objectives** (3–4 bullets, measurable).
4. **Proposed solution:** modules, a **block diagram**, and the main technology choices with one reason each.
5. **Implementation plan:** phases with rough durations (a small Gantt-style table).
6. **Cost and benefit** (rough, by category), and **risks with mitigations**.
7. **Conclusion / recommendation.**

**Worked outline for CE 2078 Q9 (Transcription prompt; outline is original):**

| Part | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Problem | Paper attendance and manual payroll lead to errors, delays and no audit trail; registration is repeated per office |
| Objectives | single staff record; biometric/RFID attendance feeding payroll automatically; payslips and tax deduction computed by rule; online registration |
| Solution | modules: Staff registry, Attendance (device + mobile check-in), Leave, Payroll (allowances, deductions, tax, bank transfer file), Reports; web app + central DB; role-based access |
| Integration | export to the government accounting system and the bank, using their published formats (state as "to be confirmed with the agency") |
| Plan | requirements and SRS 1 month · build 3 months · pilot in one unit 1 month · rollout and training 1 month |
| Risks | resistance to change → training and a parallel run; device failure → manual override with approval log; data privacy → access control, encryption, backups |
| Benefit | payroll time cut from days to hours; fewer errors; reports on demand; a base for e-governance |

### Template B: views essay or critique (about 16 minutes)

1. **Thesis** in the first two lines: your position.
2. **Context**: the situation in Nepal, briefly.
3. **3–4 arguments**, each a short paragraph with a concrete example.
4. **Counter-view / challenges**: digital divide, connectivity, cost, cybersecurity, capacity.
5. **Recommendations**: 3–5 actionable, prioritised points.
6. **Conclusion** restating the thesis.

For CE 2079 Q9, for example: direct challenges (telemedicine, contact tracing, vaccine registration and certificates, data dashboards); indirect ones (remote learning, digital payments, e-commerce for small businesses, remote work for government services); then the limits (rural connectivity, device access, digital literacy, misinformation, privacy).

| Clear structure (template followed, headings) | 2 |
| Own reasoning and judgement, not a list of facts | 3 |
| Technical soundness of the proposal or arguments | 3 |
| Feasibility, risks or counter-views addressed | 1 |
| Diagram or table that carries content | 1 |

## 10. Original practice set

All questions below are **Original practice**. They are written in PSC style, and none comes from an exam. Time tags assume 1.6 min per mark.

1. **(5 · 8 min)** Differentiate a hub, a switch and a router with respect to OSI layer, collision domain and broadcast domain. *CE 1.2–1.3 / CO 7.4*
2. **(10 · 16 min)** 172.16.0.0/16 must be divided for departments needing 500, 250, 120 and 60 hosts. Allocate subnets using VLSM; give network, range and broadcast for each. *CE 1.3 / CO 7.6*
3. **(2 + 8 · 16 min)** What is a critical section? Solve the bounded-buffer producer–consumer problem with semaphores and explain why the order of the `wait` calls matters. *CE 10.1*
4. **(10 · 16 min)** R(A, B, C, D, E) with A → B, B → C, CD → E. Find the candidate key(s), the highest normal form, and decompose to BCNF, checking whether it is lossless and dependency-preserving. *CE 9.1 / CO 5.12*
5. **(5 + 5 · 16 min)** Insert 10, 20, 30, 25, 28, 27 into an AVL tree, showing each rotation. Then state the height bound of an AVL tree with n nodes. *CE 7.3 / CO 2.4*
6. **(10 · 16 min)** Explain the two-phase locking protocol and show, with a schedule of two transactions, how strict 2PL prevents cascading rollback. *CE 9.2 / CO 5.13*
7. **(10 · 16 min, technical writing)** "Every local level in Nepal should run its own data centre." Give your views and critique, with an alternative. *CE 16.1*
8. **(20 · 32 min, case study)** A municipality issues building permits: applicant submits drawings, an engineer checks them, the ward chair recommends, the executive officer approves, and fees are paid at a bank. (a) Feasibility (technical, economic, operational) (6); (b) context and level-0 DFD (8); (c) ER diagram with keys (6). *CE 16.2 / CO 3*
