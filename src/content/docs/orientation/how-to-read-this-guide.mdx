This guide has 324 articles. Nobody should read them the way they read a novel, front to back
in one go, and almost nobody needs all of them at once. What you need is a map, a way to choose
a route through it, a way to check that you actually understood what you read, and a way to pick
up where you left off. This article gives you all four.

## The question

How do you get through a large technical guide so that the knowledge stays, instead of
evaporating a week after you close the tab?

## The map

The guide is built in three layers.

- **Seven parts**, from foundations through single-machine execution, optimization, distributed
  execution, lakehouse storage and real engines, to building and measuring your own.
- **Twenty-two modules**, each a coherent topic such as joins, aggregation or shuffles. Each
  module's overview page lists its goal, what you will be able to do afterwards, and which
  modules to read first.
- **Articles**, each covering one idea in depth, in reading order within the module.

Those "read first" links form a graph. Most modules depend on a few earlier ones, and nothing
depends on something later. The graph below is drawn from the same data as the rest of the site.
Tap a module to see everything it needs and everything it leads to, or pick one of the
suggested routes.

<PrereqGraph />

Modules on the same step don't depend on each other, so you can read them in any order. A
module two steps down can wait until you need it.

## Choosing a route

There are three good ways through.

**Start at the beginning** if you are new to databases. The "New to databases" route covers what
a query means, how data is laid out, and how one machine runs scans, joins and aggregations.
That is enough to understand why most analytical queries are fast or slow.

**Follow a route for your goal** if you already work with data systems. The "Working data
engineer" route goes straight to execution models, joins and the optimizer, then into shuffles,
distributed joins and table formats: the parts that explain the behaviour of Spark, Trino and
lakehouse tables. The "Preparing for engine roles" route goes deep on the machine, execution,
memory and shuffle, and ends with building an engine yourself, which is the depth interviews for
query execution and runtime roles tend to probe.

**Jump in where you are curious** and follow "read first" links only when an article assumes
something you don't know. This is the fastest way to answer a specific question, and the
module overviews are designed for it.

## What every article contains

Articles share one structure, so you always know where you are:

1. **The question** the article answers, in plain words.
2. **The idea**: the mechanism from first principles, usually with an interactive figure.
3. **A worked example** with concrete data, often a step-through visual.
4. **In real engines**: how DataFusion, DuckDB, Spark, Trino and others actually do it, with
   links to papers and source code.
5. **Costs and trade-offs**: when it wins, when it loses, and what that depends on.
6. **In Rust**: a short, runnable program, kept in the lab so you can run it yourself.
7. **Check yourself**: a few questions, with answers hidden until you open them.

Two conventions run through all of them. Claims about performance always say what was measured,
on what, and by whom. When something is a prediction or a hypothesis rather than a measurement,
the text says so. Boxes headed "Reading the numbers honestly" spell out what a number does and
does not show.

## Why every article ends with questions

It is tempting to skip the questions. The research on learning says they are the most valuable
part of the article.

In a well-known 2006 experiment, Roediger and Karpicke gave students a short prose passage. Some
read it four times. Others read it once and then tried to write down everything they remembered,
three times, with no feedback. A few minutes later, the students who had reread it recalled the
most. A week later the result had flipped:

<TestingEffect />

The students who practised recalling kept half again as much as the ones who reread. And the
rereaders were the most confident they would remember. Rereading feels productive because the
text becomes familiar; familiarity is not the same as being able to use it.

A 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues compared ten common study techniques across hundreds of
studies. Two came out with the highest rating for usefulness: **practice testing** and
**distributed practice**, meaning spreading study out over time. Rereading, highlighting and
summarising came out among the least useful.

So, three habits that make this guide work much better:

- **Answer before you open.** Commit to an answer in your head, or on paper, before revealing it.
  A wrong guess you then correct is still practice.
- **Come back later.** Revisit the questions from earlier articles a few days later, without
  rereading the article first. The module pages make this quick.
- **Do the lab.** Running and changing the code is retrieval practice too: you have to
  reconstruct the idea to make it work.

## Labs

The lab is a Rust workspace in the [guide's repository](https://github.com/unikdahal/learning-guide/tree/main/lab).
Every "In Rust" section is a real program in it, compiled and run against the same data the
article describes. The [next article](../setting-up-the-rust-lab/) sets it up, and the one after
that, [Measuring things honestly](../measuring-things-honestly/), sets the rules every lab follows
when it reports a number.

## Picking up where you left off

The site remembers your reading, on this device:

- On the home page, a **Continue reading** card takes you back to the section you were in, or to
  the next article once you finish one.
- When you return to an article you didn't finish, it offers to jump back to where you were.
- In the menu, written articles you have started show a **half-filled dot**, and finished ones a
  **filled dot**. Articles that are planned but not yet written show an **open ring**.
- A thin bar at the top of the screen shows how far through the current article you are.

<Aside type="note" title="Where your progress is stored">
Progress lives in your browser's local storage, not in cookies and not on any server. The site is
static, hosted on GitHub Pages, so there is no server that could read it. That also means it
stays on one device and one browser: a private window, a different phone or clearing the site's
data starts fresh. The Continue reading card on the home page has a button to clear it.
</Aside>

## How long it takes

At about 15 minutes per article, the full guide is roughly 80 hours of reading, before labs. At
45 minutes a day that is three to four months. Each suggested route is 7 modules and between 105
and 118 articles, so roughly 26 to 30 hours. These are estimates from article counts, not
measurements of anyone's reading. The reading time shown on each written article is its word count
at 200 words a minute, plus two minutes for each interactive figure.

<details>
<summary>Why did rereading beat recall practice after five minutes, but lose after a week?</summary>

Rereading keeps the material fresh in short-term memory and makes it feel familiar, which helps
on an immediate test. Retrieval practice makes you reconstruct the material yourself, which is
harder in the moment but builds memory that lasts. The benefit only shows once the short-term
advantage has faded.
</details>

<details>
<summary>You read half an article on your phone, then open the site on your laptop. Will it offer to resume?</summary>

No. Progress is kept in the browser's local storage on the phone. Nothing is synced between
devices, because nothing is sent to a server.
</details>
