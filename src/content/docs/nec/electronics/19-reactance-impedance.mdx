## 1. Three components, three behaviours

Play with the lab first — pick each load and drag the frequency:

<PhasorLab />

**Resistor.** Ohm's law works instant by instant. Current is **in phase** with voltage, and frequency doesn't matter.

**Inductor.** It opposes changing current, and AC is always changing. Its opposition is the **inductive reactance**:

`XL = 2πfL = ωL`  (ohms)

- Grows with frequency: an inductor **blocks high frequencies**, and at DC (f = 0) XL = 0 (a short — matches lesson 15).
- Current **lags** voltage by **90°**.

**Capacitor.** It charges and discharges every cycle. The faster that happens, the more current flows. Its opposition is the **capacitive reactance**:

`XC = 1/(2πfC) = 1/(ωC)`  (ohms)

- Shrinks as frequency rises: a capacitor **passes high frequencies**; at DC XC is infinite (an open circuit — matches lesson 14).
- Current **leads** voltage by **90°**.

<Aside type="tip" title="ELI the ICE man">
**ELI:** in an inductor (L), voltage E comes before current I — current lags. **ICE:** in a capacitor (C), current I comes before voltage E — current leads.
</Aside>

**Worked examples at 50 Hz.**
- L = 0.1 H: XL = 2π × 50 × 0.1 = **31.4 Ω**. At 100 Hz it doubles to 62.8 Ω.
- C = 100 µF: XC = 1/(2π × 50 × 100 × 10⁻⁶) = **31.8 Ω**. At 100 Hz it halves to 15.9 Ω.

## 2. Impedance — combining R and X

With a resistor and a reactance in series you **can't just add the ohms**: the voltage across R is in phase with the current, but the voltage across X is 90° out. They add like the two sides of a right-angled triangle:

`|Z| = √(R² + X²)`   and   `φ = tan⁻¹(X/R)`

**Impedance** Z (ohms) is the total opposition to AC; φ is the angle between voltage and current. Then **Ohm's law works with Z**: `I = V/Z`.

**Worked example.** R = 30 Ω in series with XL = 40 Ω on 230 V, 50 Hz.
- |Z| = √(900 + 1,600) = **50 Ω** (a 3-4-5 triangle).
- I = 230/50 = **4.6 A**.
- φ = tan⁻¹(40/30) = **53.1°**, current lagging.
- Power factor cos φ = 30/50 = **0.6 lagging** (lesson 21).

### Phasors and j (just enough)

A **phasor** is an arrow whose length is the RMS value and whose angle is the phase — the lab's right-hand diagram. Adding AC quantities = adding arrows. Engineers write the 90° direction with **j** (j = √−1): `Z = R + jXL` for RL, `Z = R − jXC` for RC. "j" just means "rotate by 90°"; `|Z|` is the arrow's length.

## 3. A first look at filters

Put R and C in series and take the output across one of them:

- **Output across C → low-pass filter.** At low f, XC is large, so C gets most of the voltage; at high f, XC is small and the output drops. Passes low frequencies.
- **Output across R → high-pass filter.** The opposite.
- **Cut-off frequency** where XC = R: `fc = 1/(2πRC)`. There, the output is 0.707 of the input (**−3 dB**, half power).

**Example.** R = 1 kΩ, C = 0.1 µF → fc = 1/(2π × 10³ × 10⁻⁷) ≈ **1.6 kHz**.

## Traps

- XL ∝ f; XC ∝ **1/f**.
- Impedance adds **as vectors**: 30 Ω and 40 Ω give 50 Ω, not 70 Ω.
- Pure L or C: phase 90°. RL or RC: somewhere between 0° and 90°.
