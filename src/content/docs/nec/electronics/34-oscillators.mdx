## 1. What an oscillator is

An **oscillator** produces a repeating waveform (sine, square, triangle) from DC power, with **no input signal**. Every clock in your computer, every radio transmitter, every quartz watch has one.

It's an amplifier whose output is fed back to its own input **in phase** (positive feedback). Any tiny noise gets amplified, fed back, amplified again — and settles into a steady oscillation at the one frequency where the loop conditions are met.

## 2. The Barkhausen criterion

For sustained oscillation, around the loop (amplifier gain A, feedback network β):

1. **Loop gain magnitude** `|Aβ| = 1`.
2. **Total phase shift** around the loop = **0° or 360°**.

To **start**, |Aβ| is made slightly greater than 1; the amplitude grows until the amplifier's non-linearity trims it back to exactly 1.

## 3. RC oscillators (audio frequencies)

**RC phase-shift oscillator.** An inverting amplifier gives 180°; three RC sections give another 180° (60° each, at one frequency) → 360° total.
- `f = 1 / (2π R C √6)`
- The RC network attenuates by 1/29 there, so the amplifier needs **gain ≥ 29**.
- *Example:* R = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF → f = 1/(2π × 10⁴ × 10⁻⁸ × 2.449) ≈ **650 Hz**.

**Wien-bridge oscillator.** A series RC and a parallel RC (a lead–lag network) give **0°** shift at one frequency, with β = 1/3. A non-inverting amplifier with gain 3 sustains it.
- `f = 1 / (2π R C)`
- *Example:* same R and C → ≈ **1.59 kHz**.
- Low distortion; the classic audio test oscillator.

## 4. LC oscillators (radio frequencies)

A tank circuit (L and C) sets the frequency, `f = 1/(2π√(L C))` ([lesson 20](/learning-guide/nec/electronics/20-resonance/)); the amplifier makes up the tank's losses.

- **Hartley:** tapped **inductor** (L1, L2) with one capacitor: `L_eq = L1 + L2 + 2M`.
- **Colpitts:** tapped **capacitor** (C1, C2) with one inductor: `C_eq = C1 C2 / (C1 + C2)`.
- *Example (Colpitts):* L = 100 µH, C1 = C2 = 1 nF → C_eq = 0.5 nF; f = 1/(2π√(10⁻⁴ × 5 × 10⁻¹⁰)) ≈ **712 kHz**.

Memory trick: **Hartley — Henry (inductors); Colpitts — Capacitors.**

## 5. Crystal oscillators

A thin slice of **quartz** vibrates mechanically when a voltage is applied, and produces a voltage when it vibrates — the **piezoelectric effect**. Electrically it behaves like an RLC circuit with an extremely high Q (10⁴ to 10⁶).

- Two resonances close together: **series** (f_s) and **parallel** (f_p, slightly higher).
- Frequency stability is superb — parts per million.
- Used for every **clock**: CPUs, motherboards, network cards, watches (32.768 kHz, which divides by 2¹⁵ to give exactly 1 Hz).

## 6. Waveform generators (non-sinusoidal)

**Multivibrators** — two-state circuits named by how many stable states they have:

| Type | Stable states | Output | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Astable** | none — flips back and forth on its own | square wave | clocks, LED flashers |
| **Monostable** (one-shot) | one | a single pulse of set width when triggered | timers, debouncing |
| **Bistable** | two — stays until triggered | a stored bit | **flip-flop** (the SR flip-flop is a bistable multivibrator) |

**555 timer** (an IC that makes these easy):
- Astable: `f = 1.44 / ((R_A + 2R_B) C)`. *Example:* R_A = 1 kΩ, R_B = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF → ≈ **6.9 kHz**.
- Monostable pulse width: `T = 1.1 R C`.

**Schmitt trigger:** a comparator with hysteresis (two thresholds) that turns slow or noisy signals into clean square edges. Combine a Schmitt trigger and an integrator and you get a **square + triangle** generator; sawtooth generators drive old CRT sweeps.

**Question.** State the Barkhausen criterion. Explain the working of an RC phase-shift oscillator and give its frequency.

<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Oscillator.** A circuit that generates a periodic waveform without an input signal, using an amplifier with positive feedback.

**Barkhausen criterion.** For sustained oscillations: (1) the magnitude of the loop gain |Aβ| = 1; (2) the total phase shift around the loop is 0° or 360°. In practice |Aβ| is kept slightly above 1 at start-up. *(Draw the amplifier–feedback loop.)*

**RC phase-shift oscillator.** *(Draw a CE transistor or inverting op-amp amplifier with three cascaded RC sections from output back to input.)*
- The amplifier provides 180° phase shift.
- Each RC section provides 60°, so the three sections give 180° at one frequency.
- Total = 360°, satisfying the phase condition.
- At that frequency the network's attenuation is 1/29, so the amplifier gain must be at least 29 to satisfy |Aβ| ≥ 1.

**Frequency.** `f = 1/(2πRC√6)` for equal R and C.

**Example.** R = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF → f ≈ 650 Hz.

**Features.** Simple and cheap; good for audio frequencies; frequency tuning needs changing three components together. For RF, LC (Hartley, Colpitts) or crystal oscillators are used.
