## 1. Current makes magnetism

Any current in a wire creates a **magnetic field** around it. Wind the wire into a coil and the fields of all the turns add up. Put an iron core inside and the field gets much stronger still. That coil is an **inductor**.

## 2. Changing magnetism makes voltage

Two laws, discovered in the 1830s:

- **Faraday's law:** a *changing* magnetic field through a coil **induces a voltage** in it. The faster the change, the bigger the voltage. (A steady field induces nothing.)
- **Lenz's law:** the induced voltage always pushes **against the change** that caused it.

Put them together for a single coil: if you try to increase the current, the growing field induces a voltage that fights the increase. Try to cut the current, and the collapsing field induces a voltage that tries to keep it going. **An inductor resists changes in current** — like a heavy flywheel resists changes in speed.

## 3. Inductance: V = L di/dt

`v = L × (di/dt)`

- **L** is the **inductance**, in **henries** (H).
- **di/dt** is how fast the current changes, in amperes per second.

**Worked example.** A 2 H inductor's current rises steadily from 0 to 3 A in 0.5 s. di/dt = 3/0.5 = 6 A/s, so the induced voltage = 2 × 6 = **12 V**.

**Worked example.** The same current is switched off in 1 ms. di/dt = 3/0.001 = 3,000 A/s → v = **6,000 V**! This is why switching off an inductive load (relay coil, motor) can spark — and why circuits add a "flyback" diode across coils (Part 5).

### What makes inductance bigger

`L = μ N² A / l`

- **N** — number of turns, and it's **squared**: double the turns → **four times** the inductance.
- **A** — core cross-section; **l** — coil length.
- **μ** — permeability of the core; iron is hundreds to thousands of times better than air.

## 4. Energy stored

`E = ½ L I²`

**Worked example.** 2 H carrying 3 A stores ½ × 2 × 9 = **9 J**.

## 5. Behaviour in a circuit

- **Its current can't change instantly.**
- **On steady DC it acts like a short circuit** (just a piece of wire, apart from the wire's small resistance).
- **On AC it opposes current**, more so at higher frequency (lesson 19).

## 6. Series and parallel — like resistors

(Assuming the coils' fields don't link each other.)

- Series: `L = L1 + L2 + …`
- Parallel: `1/L = 1/L1 + 1/L2 + …`

## 7. The mirror — memorise half, get both

| | Capacitor | Inductor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stores energy in | electric field | magnetic field |
| Defining equation | `i = C dv/dt` | `v = L di/dt` |
| Can't change instantly | **voltage** | **current** |
| Steady DC | open circuit | short circuit |
| Energy | `½CV²` | `½LI²` |
| Series | reciprocal rule | add |
| Parallel | add | reciprocal rule |
| AC current | **leads** voltage by 90° | **lags** voltage by 90° |

## 8. Head start for Lok Sewa: the transformer

Two coils on one iron core. AC in the first (primary) makes a changing field, which induces a voltage in the second (secondary) — Faraday again.

- `Vs / Vp = Ns / Np` (turns ratio). More secondary turns → step **up** the voltage.
- Power is (ideally) conserved, so current goes the other way: `Is / Ip = Np / Ns`.
- **Works only on AC** — DC gives a steady field, and a steady field induces nothing.
- That's why grids use AC: step up to tens of kV for low-loss transmission (less current → less I²R loss), step down to 230 V at your house. Your laptop charger has a small one inside.

**Example.** 230 V primary with 1,000 turns, 50-turn secondary → 230 × 50/1,000 = **11.5 V**.

## Traps

- Inductors in series **add** (like resistors), unlike capacitors.
- Turns are **squared** in `L = μN²A/l`.
- Steady DC: inductor = **short circuit**.
- Transformers don't work on DC.
