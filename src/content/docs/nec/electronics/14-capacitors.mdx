## 1. What a capacitor is

Take two metal plates and hold them close together **without touching**, with an insulator (air, paper, ceramic, plastic) between them. Connect a battery:

- The battery pulls electrons off one plate and pushes them onto the other.
- One plate ends up with **+Q**, the other with **−Q**. No charge crosses the gap — the insulator stops it.
- Charging continues until the plates' voltage equals the battery's. Then current stops.

The separated charges create an **electric field** across the gap, and that field holds **energy**. That's all a capacitor is: a device that stores energy by keeping charges apart.

## 2. Capacitance: Q = CV

The charge stored is proportional to the voltage:

`Q = C × V`

**C** is the **capacitance**, measured in **farads** (F). One farad stores one coulomb per volt. That's enormous; real parts are µF, nF and pF.

**Worked example.** A 100 µF capacitor on 12 V stores Q = 100 × 10⁻⁶ × 12 = **1.2 mC**.

### What makes capacitance bigger

`C = ε A / d`

- **A** — plate area: bigger plates hold more charge.
- **d** — gap: closer plates → bigger C.
- **ε** (epsilon) — permittivity of the insulator, `ε = ε₀ εr`, with ε₀ ≈ 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m and εr the **dielectric constant** (air ≈ 1, ceramics up to thousands).

Double the area → double C. Halve the gap → double C.

## 3. Energy stored

`E = ½ C V²`  (joules)

**Worked example.** The same 100 µF at 12 V: E = ½ × 100 × 10⁻⁶ × 144 = **7.2 mJ**. Small — but a camera flash capacitor at 300 V holds far more, which is why big capacitors can shock you even when unplugged.

## 4. How a capacitor behaves in a circuit

- **Its voltage can't change instantly.** Changing it means moving charge, which takes time (lesson 16). Current: `i = C × dv/dt`.
- **On steady DC it acts like an open circuit**: once charged, no current flows.
- **On AC it lets current through**: it keeps charging and discharging, and the faster the change, the easier (lesson 19).

That pair — blocks DC, passes AC — is why capacitors **couple** signals between amplifier stages and **filter** ripple out of power supplies.

## 5. Series and parallel — opposite to resistors

| | Capacitors | (Resistors, for comparison) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Parallel** | `C = C1 + C2 + …` (plate area adds) | reciprocal rule |
| **Series** | `1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …` (gap effectively grows) | add |
| Two in series | `C1C2 / (C1 + C2)` | — |
| Shared in series | same **charge** on each | same current |
| Shared in parallel | same **voltage** | same voltage |

**Worked example.** 6 µF and 3 µF: parallel = **9 µF**; series = 18/9 = **2 µF**.

**Worked example.** In series on 12 V, the smaller capacitor gets the *bigger* voltage (same Q, and V = Q/C). With 6 µF and 3 µF in series: Q = 2 µF × 12 V = 24 µC; V across 6 µF = 24/6 = 4 V, across 3 µF = 24/3 = 8 V.

## 6. Where you'll meet them

- **Electrolytic** (large µF, polarised — fitting one backwards can make it burst), **ceramic** (small, non-polarised), **film**.
- Power-supply smoothing after a rectifier (Part 5).
- **Decoupling capacitors** beside every chip on a motherboard or server board, absorbing tiny voltage dips when the chip suddenly draws current.
- Timing circuits (Part 6) and DRAM cells (one tiny capacitor per bit, refreshed because it leaks).

## Traps

- Capacitors in **series** use the reciprocal rule — opposite to resistors.
- In series, the **smaller** capacitor takes the **larger** voltage.
- `E = ½CV²`, not `CV²`.
- Steady DC: capacitor = open circuit.

   (a) 0.25 J (b) 0.5 J (c) 5 mJ (d) 2.5 J
