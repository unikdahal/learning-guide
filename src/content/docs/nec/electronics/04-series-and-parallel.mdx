## 1. Series

Components are in **series** when they're connected end to end with **no branch** between them, so there's only **one path**.

- **Same current** through every component.
- **Voltages add up:** `V = V1 + V2 + …` (the source's push is shared out).
- **Resistances add:** `R_total = R1 + R2 + …` — always **bigger** than the largest one.
- One break anywhere stops everything (old-style festival lights).

**Voltage divider** (two resistors in series across V):

`V1 = V × R1 / (R1 + R2)`

The bigger resistor gets the bigger share of the voltage.

**Worked example.** 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 5 Ω in series across 20 V.

- R_total = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 Ω
- I = 20 / 10 = 2 A (the same through all three)
- Drops: 2 × 2 = 4 V, 2 × 3 = 6 V, 2 × 5 = 10 V. Check: 4 + 6 + 10 = 20 V. ✔

## 2. Parallel

Components are in **parallel** when both ends of each are connected to the **same two points**.

- **Same voltage** across every component.
- **Currents add up:** `I = I1 + I2 + …` (the current splits between branches).
- **Reciprocals add:** `1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …` — always **smaller** than the smallest one.
- Two resistors: `R_total = R1 × R2 / (R1 + R2)` ("product over sum").
- n equal resistors R: `R_total = R / n`.
- Houses are wired in parallel: every appliance gets the full 230 V, and switching one off doesn't affect the others.

**Current divider** (two branches sharing current I):

`I1 = I × R2 / (R1 + R2)`

Note the **other** resistor on top: more current takes the easier (smaller-resistance) path.

**Worked example.** 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel across 12 V.

- R_total = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3) = 18 / 9 = 2 Ω
- Branch currents: 12 / 6 = 2 A and 12 / 3 = 4 A. Total 6 A.
- Check: 12 V / 2 Ω = 6 A. ✔

## 3. Mixed networks — reduce step by step

Find a group that's purely series or purely parallel, replace it with one resistor, redraw, repeat.

**Worked example.** 4 Ω in series with (6 Ω ∥ 3 Ω), across 12 V. Find every current.

1. 6 ∥ 3 = 2 Ω.
2. Now 4 + 2 = 6 Ω total.
3. Supply current I = 12 / 6 = **2 A** (it all flows through the 4 Ω).
4. Voltage across the 4 Ω = 2 × 4 = 8 V, so the parallel pair gets 12 − 8 = **4 V**.
5. Branch currents: 4 / 6 = **0.67 A** and 4 / 3 = **1.33 A** (they add to 2 A ✔).

<Aside type="tip" title="Sanity checks that catch most errors">
Series total must be bigger than every part. Parallel total must be smaller than every branch. Branch currents must add back to the supply current.
</Aside>

## 4. Short and open circuits

- A **short circuit** across a resistor (a wire with ~0 Ω in parallel) takes all the current: the resistor carries nothing and has 0 V across it.
- An **open circuit** (a break, ∞ Ω) carries no current, but the full voltage can appear across the gap.

## 5. Power in series and parallel

Total power is always the **sum** of individual powers, whichever way they're connected.

A classic question: two identical bulbs on the same supply — which arrangement is brighter? In **parallel**, each bulb gets the full voltage, so P = V²/R each. In **series**, each gets V/2, so each glows at **a quarter** of that power.

## Traps

- "In series, voltage is the same" — no: **current** is the same in series; **voltage** is the same in parallel.
- Putting the wrong resistor on top in the current divider.
- Forgetting that adding a resistor **in parallel always lowers** the total resistance.

## Check yourself

1. In a series circuit, the current:
   (a) is the same through each component (b) is different in each (c) divides between components (d) is zero in the largest resistor

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) one path, so the same current everywhere.</details>

2. Three 9 Ω resistors in parallel have an equivalent resistance of:
   (a) 27 Ω (b) 9 Ω (c) 3 Ω (d) 1/3 Ω

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(c) R/n = 9/3 = 3 Ω.</details>

3. 10 Ω and 40 Ω in series across 100 V. The voltage across the 40 Ω is:
   (a) 20 V (b) 50 V (c) 80 V (d) 100 V

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(c) Divider: 100 × 40/50 = 80 V.</details>

4. 12 A splits between 4 Ω and 2 Ω in parallel. Current in the 4 Ω branch?
   (a) 8 A (b) 6 A (c) 4 A (d) 3 A

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(c) 12 × 2/(4 + 2) = 4 A. The 2 Ω branch takes 8 A.</details>

5. Adding another resistor in parallel to an existing network:
   (a) increases total resistance (b) decreases total resistance (c) leaves it unchanged (d) makes it infinite

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) it always gives current another path.</details>

6. 3 Ω in series with (4 Ω ∥ 12 Ω). Total resistance?
   (a) 19 Ω (b) 6 Ω (c) 4.5 Ω (d) 3 Ω

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) 4 ∥ 12 = 48/16 = 3 Ω; 3 + 3 = 6 Ω.</details>

7. Household appliances are connected in parallel mainly because:
   (a) it uses less wire (b) each gets the full supply voltage and works independently (c) it reduces total current (d) it increases resistance

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) same voltage across each, and each can be switched separately.</details>
