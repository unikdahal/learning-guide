## 1. What a filter does

A filter passes some frequencies and weakens (**attenuates**) others. You already have the parts: a capacitor's reactance falls with frequency; an inductor's rises. Put them in a voltage divider with a resistor and the output depends on frequency.

| Filter | Passes | Blocks | Simplest circuit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Low-pass** | low frequencies | high | R in series, **C across the output** (or L in series, R across output) |
| **High-pass** | high | low | C in series, **R across the output** |
| **Band-pass** | a band around f₀ | both sides | series RLC, output across R |
| **Band-stop (notch)** | everything except a band | the band | parallel LC trap |

Everyday uses: a speaker crossover sends lows to the woofer (low-pass) and highs to the tweeter (high-pass); a power supply's capacitor is a low-pass that removes ripple; a radio's tuned circuit is a band-pass; a 50 Hz notch removes mains hum from ECG signals.

## 2. Decibels

Ratios in electronics span huge ranges, so they're expressed in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale:

- Power ratio: `gain (dB) = 10 log₁₀(P₂/P₁)`
- Voltage ratio (same impedance): `gain (dB) = 20 log₁₀(V₂/V₁)` — the 20 comes from P ∝ V².

| Ratio | Power gain | Voltage gain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| × 2 | +3 dB | +6 dB |
| × 10 | +10 dB | +20 dB |
| × 100 | +20 dB | +40 dB |
| × ½ | −3 dB | −6 dB |
| × 0.707 (voltage) | −3 dB (half power) | −3 dB |

Gains in a chain of stages **add** in dB (multiply as ratios).

<Aside type="caution" title="The model-paper question">
NEC's model paper asks for the decibel relation for power gain written with voltages and impedances: `N_dB = 20 log(V₂/V₁) + 10 log(Z₁/Z₂)`. It follows from P = V²/Z: 10 log((V₂²/Z₂)/(V₁²/Z₁)). When Z₁ = Z₂ the second term vanishes.
</Aside>

## 3. The first-order RC low-pass filter

Output taken across C in an R–C divider.

- **Cut-off (corner) frequency** where XC = R: **`fc = 1 / (2πRC)`**.
- Gain magnitude: `|Vout/Vin| = 1 / √(1 + (f/fc)²)`.
- At fc the gain is 1/√2 = 0.707 = **−3 dB** (half power), and the output lags the input by **45°**.

| f | Gain | dB |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.1 fc | 0.995 | ≈ 0 |
| fc | 0.707 | −3 |
| 10 fc | 0.0995 | −20 |
| 100 fc | 0.01 | −40 |

Above fc the gain falls by **20 dB per decade** (each tenfold rise in frequency cuts the output tenfold) — the signature of a **first-order** filter. Second-order (e.g. RLC) filters roll off at 40 dB/decade.

**High-pass** is the mirror: swap R and C, same fc, gain `(f/fc)/√(1 + (f/fc)²)`, rising 20 dB/decade below fc.

**Worked example.** Design a low-pass with fc ≈ 1 kHz using C = 100 nF.
R = 1/(2π × 1,000 × 10⁻⁷) ≈ **1.59 kΩ**. At 10 kHz the output is about −20 dB (one tenth).

**RL version.** L in series, R across the output: low-pass with `fc = R / (2πL)`.

## 4. Band-pass and bandwidth

A series RLC with output across R passes frequencies near f₀ = 1/(2π√LC) — exactly the resonance curve of [lesson 20](/learning-guide/nec/electronics/20-resonance/). The **bandwidth** between the two −3 dB points is f₀/Q.

## 5. Bode plots, in one paragraph

A **Bode plot** draws gain in dB against frequency on a log scale (plus a phase plot). On it, a first-order low-pass is a flat line that bends at fc and then falls in a straight line at −20 dB/decade. Engineers sketch these "asymptotes" by hand to estimate a circuit's response in seconds.

## 6. Passive vs active filters

**Passive** filters (R, L, C only) can't amplify and get loaded by what follows. **Active** filters add an op-amp (Part 6): gain, buffering and no inductors needed — the norm at audio frequencies.

**Question.** What is a filter? Explain a first-order RC low-pass filter with its cut-off frequency and frequency response.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Definition.** A filter is a frequency-selective circuit that passes signals in a desired band and attenuates others. Types: low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, band-stop.

**RC low-pass.** R in series with the input; C across the output. *(Draw.)* At low frequency X_C is large, so most of the input appears across C; at high frequency X_C is small and the output falls.

**Cut-off frequency.** At f_c = 1/(2πRC), X_C = R, the output is 0.707 of the input (−3 dB, half power), and lags by 45°.

**Frequency response.** Gain = 1/√(1 + (f/f_c)²): flat below f_c, then falling at 20 dB per decade. *(Sketch gain in dB vs log f.)*

**Use.** Removing high-frequency noise, smoothing power-supply ripple, anti-aliasing before sampling.

</details>
