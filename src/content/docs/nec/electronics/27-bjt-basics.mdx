## 1. Structure

Three doped layers, two junctions:

- **NPN:** n–p–n. **PNP:** p–n–p.
- **Emitter (E):** heavily doped — it **emits** the carriers.
- **Base (B):** very **thin** and lightly doped — the control region.
- **Collector (C):** moderately doped and the **largest** — it **collects** carriers and dissipates most of the heat.
- Two junctions: base–emitter (BE) and collector–base (CB).

Memory trick: **NPN — "Not Pointing iN"**.

## 2. How it works (NPN, active region)

Forward-bias BE (≈ 0.7 V) and reverse-bias CB:

1. The forward-biased BE junction **injects** a flood of electrons from the emitter into the base.
2. The base is thin and lightly doped, so only a **few** of them recombine there — that small flow out of the base terminal is **I_B**.
3. The rest reach the CB junction, where its strong reverse field **sweeps them into the collector**: that's **I_C**, nearly all of the emitter current.

So a **small base current controls a large collector current**. That's amplification.

## 3. The three currents

`I_E = I_B + I_C` (KCL — the transistor is just a node)

- **α (alpha)** = I_C / I_E — the common-base current gain, just under 1 (0.95–0.995).
- **β (beta, or h_FE)** = I_C / I_B — the common-emitter current gain, typically 50–300.
- They're linked: **`β = α / (1 − α)`** and **`α = β / (1 + β)`**.

**Worked example.** I_B = 40 µA and β = 100. Then I_C = 4 mA, I_E = 4.04 mA, α = 4/4.04 = **0.990**. Check: 0.99/(1 − 0.99) = 99 ≈ 100 ✔.

**Leakage.** With the emitter open, a tiny current I_CBO flows across the reverse-biased CB junction. In common-emitter it's amplified: `I_CEO = (1 + β) I_CBO`. It doubles roughly every 10 °C — the root of thermal runaway (lesson 28).

## 4. Operating regions

| Region | BE junction | CB junction | Behaviour | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cut-off** | reverse | reverse | I_C ≈ 0; switch OFF | digital "0"/open switch |
| **Active** | **forward** | **reverse** | I_C = β I_B | **amplifier** |
| **Saturation** | forward | forward | I_C at its max, V_CE ≈ 0.2 V; switch ON | digital "1"/closed switch |
| Reverse active | reverse | forward | poor gain | rarely used |

## 5. Characteristics (common emitter)

- **Input characteristic** (I_B vs V_BE): looks just like a diode — nothing until ≈ 0.7 V, then a steep rise.
- **Output characteristics** (I_C vs V_CE, one curve per I_B): after a small knee (saturation, V_CE ≈ 0.2 V), each curve is **nearly flat** — I_C depends on I_B, hardly on V_CE. They tilt up slightly because the effective base narrows as V_CE rises (the **Early effect**). Below the I_B = 0 curve is cut-off. Next lesson's lab draws these.

## 6. Three configurations

Which terminal is common to input and output names the configuration. Each has three terminals in use; one is shared.

| | Common emitter (CE) | Common base (CB) | Common collector (CC) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Input → output | base → collector | emitter → collector | base → emitter |
| Current gain | high (β) | below 1 (α) | high (β + 1) |
| Voltage gain | high | high | ≈ 1 |
| Power gain | **highest** | moderate | low |
| Input impedance | medium (≈ 1 kΩ) | **low** (≈ 20 Ω) | **high** (hundreds of kΩ) |
| Output impedance | medium–high | **high** | **low** |
| Phase shift | **180°** | 0° | 0° |
| Typical use | general amplifier | high-frequency, current buffer | **buffer**, impedance matching ("emitter follower") |

## Lok Sewa: write it (10 marks · 16 minutes)

**Question.** Explain the working of an NPN transistor in the active region. Derive the relation between α and β, and compare CE, CB and CC configurations.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Structure.** An NPN transistor has a heavily doped n-type emitter, a thin lightly doped p-type base and a moderately doped, larger n-type collector, forming the BE and CB junctions. *(Draw the structure and symbol.)*

**Working (active region).** BE is forward biased, CB reverse biased. Electrons are injected from the emitter into the base. Because the base is thin and lightly doped, only a small fraction (2–5%) recombine with holes, forming the base current I_B. The rest diffuse to the CB junction and are swept by its field into the collector, forming I_C. Thus `I_E = I_B + I_C`, and a small I_B controls a large I_C.

**α–β relation.** α = I_C/I_E, β = I_C/I_B. Dividing I_E = I_B + I_C by I_C: 1/α = 1/β + 1 → **β = α/(1 − α)**, and **α = β/(1 + β)**. Example: α = 0.98 → β = 49.

**Comparison of configurations.**

| | CB | CE | CC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Current gain | α < 1 | β (high) | 1 + β |
| Voltage gain | high | high | ≈ 1 |
| Input / output impedance | low / high | medium / medium | high / low |
| Phase shift | 0° | 180° | 0° |
| Application | high-frequency amplifier | general amplifier | buffer |

**Conclusion.** CE is the most widely used because it gives the highest power gain.

</details>
