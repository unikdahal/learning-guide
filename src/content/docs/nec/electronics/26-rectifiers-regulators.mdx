## 1. The DC power supply, as blocks

Every laptop charger, phone charger and server PSU does this (modern ones with switching tricks, but the same idea):

**230 V AC → transformer (step down) → rectifier (AC → pulsing DC) → filter (smooth it) → regulator (hold it exactly) → load**

## 2. Three rectifiers

Try them first — switch between half-wave and bridge, then add a capacitor:

<RectifierLab />

**Half-wave:** one diode. Conducts on positive half-cycles only; the output is zero for half the time.

**Centre-tap full-wave:** two diodes and a transformer with a centre tap on its secondary. Each diode handles one half-cycle; both halves come out positive.

**Bridge full-wave:** four diodes in a bridge, no centre tap needed. Two diodes conduct on each half-cycle, so the output loses two diode drops (≈ 1.4 V).

| Figure (Vm = peak input to the diodes) | Half-wave | Centre-tap | Bridge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diodes | 1 | 2 | 4 |
| DC (average) output | Vm/π = 0.318 Vm | 2Vm/π = 0.637 Vm | 2Vm/π |
| RMS output | Vm/2 | Vm/√2 | Vm/√2 |
| **Ripple frequency** | **f** (50 Hz) | **2f** (100 Hz) | **2f** |
| **Peak inverse voltage (PIV)** | **Vm** | **2Vm** | **Vm** |
| Ripple factor | 1.21 | 0.482 | 0.482 |
| Max efficiency | 40.6% | 81.2% | 81.2% |
| Transformer utilisation factor | 0.287 | 0.693 | 0.812 |

*(Vm for the centre-tap is the peak of each half of the secondary.)*

**Ripple factor** γ = (RMS of the AC part) / (DC value) = √((V_rms/V_dc)² − 1). Lower is smoother. **PIV** is the largest reverse voltage a diode must survive — choose diodes rated above it.

**Why the bridge is the usual choice:** full-wave output and the low PIV of Vm, with no centre-tapped transformer. Its cost: four diodes and two diode drops.

## 3. Smoothing with a capacitor

A large capacitor across the load charges to the peak each pulse, then supplies the load between pulses, discharging slightly. The leftover wobble is the **ripple**:

`V_ripple (peak-to-peak) ≈ I_load / (f_r C) = V_peak / (f_r R C)`

where f_r is the **ripple frequency** (50 Hz half-wave, 100 Hz full-wave).

**Worked example.** A bridge gives V_peak = 10.6 V into R = 1 kΩ (≈ 10.6 mA) with C = 470 µF:
V_ripple ≈ 10.6 / (100 × 1,000 × 470 × 10⁻⁶) = 10.6/47 ≈ **0.23 V**. With a half-wave rectifier, f_r = 50 Hz, so ripple **doubles**.

Bigger C, lighter load or full-wave → less ripple. Other filters: an inductor in series (choke input, LC) or a π filter (C–L–C) for smoother output.

## 4. The Zener regulator

Ripple and load changes still move the voltage. A Zener diode in reverse breakdown holds an almost fixed voltage:

- Series resistor R_s from the unregulated supply, Zener (reverse) across the load.
- `V_out ≈ V_Z`. Extra current goes through the Zener; when the load draws more, the Zener takes less.
- Design: `R_s = (V_in(min) − V_Z) / (I_L(max) + I_Z(min))`.

**Worked example.** V_in = 12 V, V_Z = 5.1 V, load 20 mA, keep at least 5 mA in the Zener: R_s = (12 − 5.1)/(0.025) = **276 Ω** → use 270 Ω.

**IC regulators** do the job better: the **78xx** series gives fixed positive outputs (7805 → +5 V, 7812 → +12 V), 79xx negative; LM317 is adjustable. They need the input a couple of volts above the output.

## 5. Other diode circuits (briefly)

- **Clipper (limiter):** diodes cut off the part of a waveform above or below a level — input protection.
- **Clamper (DC restorer):** a capacitor and diode shift a waveform up or down without changing its shape.
- **Voltage doubler:** diodes and capacitors stack charges to get about 2Vm DC.
- **Flyback (freewheeling) diode** across a relay or motor coil absorbs the voltage spike when it switches off ([lesson 15](/learning-guide/nec/electronics/15-inductors/)).

## Lok Sewa: write it (10 marks · 16 minutes)

**Question.** Explain the working of a full-wave bridge rectifier with circuit and waveforms. Derive its average output voltage and state its PIV, ripple frequency and efficiency.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Circuit.** Four diodes D1–D4 in a bridge; the AC (transformer secondary) connects across one diagonal and the load R_L across the other. *(Draw.)*

**Working.** During the positive half-cycle, D1 and D2 are forward biased and conduct; current flows through R_L from top to bottom. During the negative half-cycle, D3 and D4 conduct and current flows through R_L **in the same direction**. Hence both halves appear positive across the load. *(Draw input sine and output full-wave pulses.)*

**Average output.** `V_dc = (1/π) ∫₀^π V_m sin θ dθ = (V_m/π)[−cos θ]₀^π = 2V_m/π ≈ 0.637 V_m` (ignoring diode drops; with drops, use V_m − 1.4 V).

**Figures.**
- PIV per diode = V_m.
- Ripple frequency = 2f (100 Hz for 50 Hz mains).
- RMS output = V_m/√2; ripple factor = 0.482; maximum efficiency = 81.2%.

**Advantages.** Full-wave output without a centre-tapped transformer; low PIV; better transformer utilisation (0.812). **Disadvantages.** Four diodes; two diode drops.

**Filtering.** A capacitor across R_L smooths the output; ripple ≈ V_peak/(2fR_LC).

</details>
