**Goal:** compute P, Q and S for any AC load, use the power triangle, and explain power factor and its correction.

<Aside type="note" title="Where this scores for you">
**NEC 1.2** (active and reactive power) — and the model paper asks the **maximum value of power factor**. **Lok Sewa Computer Engineer 4.1.2**. In your day job: UPS and server power supplies are rated in **VA** and **W** separately — this lesson is why.
</Aside>

## 1. Where the power goes

Multiply voltage and current moment by moment: `p = v × i`.

- **Resistor:** v and i are in phase, so p is always positive — energy flows one way and becomes heat.
- **Pure inductor or capacitor:** 90° out of phase, so p is positive for a quarter cycle and negative for the next. Energy is **borrowed and returned** every cycle; on average **zero** power is used. But current still flows in the wires to carry that energy back and forth.

## 2. Three kinds of power

With RMS values V and I and phase angle φ:

| Name | Formula | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Real (active, true) power** P | `V I cos φ` | watt (W) | actually converted to heat, light, motion |
| **Reactive power** Q | `V I sin φ` | volt-ampere reactive (VAR) | shuttled to and from L and C |
| **Apparent power** S | `V I` | volt-ampere (VA) | what the wiring and source must carry |

They form a right-angled triangle:

<Figure caption="The power triangle: real power along the base, reactive power up the side, apparent power on the hypotenuse.">
  <Circuit name="power-triangle" label="Right-angled triangle. Base: real power P equals V I cos phi in watts. Vertical side: reactive power Q equals V I sin phi in VAR. Hypotenuse: apparent power S equals V I in VA. The angle phi is between the base and the hypotenuse." />
</Figure>

`S² = P² + Q²`

<Aside type="caution" title="A common wrong answer">
Total ("apparent") power is **not** P + Q added like ordinary numbers — some practice banks get this wrong. It's the hypotenuse: S = √(P² + Q²).
</Aside>

## 3. Power factor

`power factor = cos φ = P / S = R / |Z|`

- Ranges from 0 to **1** (its maximum: a purely resistive load, φ = 0).
- **Lagging** — current lags; inductive loads: motors, fans, transformers, fluorescent ballasts. Most real loads.
- **Leading** — current leads; capacitive loads.

**Worked example.** A motor on 230 V draws 10 A at pf 0.8 lagging.
- S = 230 × 10 = **2,300 VA**
- P = 2,300 × 0.8 = **1,840 W**
- sin φ = √(1 − 0.64) = 0.6, so Q = 2,300 × 0.6 = **1,380 VAR**
- Check: √(1,840² + 1,380²) = 2,300 ✔.

**Your-world example.** A 1,000 VA UPS with output pf 0.8 can supply at most **800 W**. The VA rating limits current (wiring, semiconductors); the W rating is the real work done.

## 4. Why low power factor is bad

For the same real power P, current `I = P / (V cos φ)`. At pf 0.5 you need **twice** the current of pf 1.0:

- I²R losses in cables and transformers rise **four times**.
- Cables, transformers and generators must be bigger.
- So utilities charge large industrial consumers for poor power factor.

## 5. Power-factor correction

Inductive loads draw lagging reactive power. Connect **capacitors in parallel** with them: capacitors supply leading reactive power locally, cancelling much of the inductive Q. Real power is unchanged; current from the supply falls; cos φ rises towards 1.

## Traps

- **Maximum** power factor = 1, at φ = 0 (pure resistance).
- `P = VI cos φ` — only **real** power is in watts.
- **Capacitors in parallel** correct a lagging power factor (not series, not inductors).
