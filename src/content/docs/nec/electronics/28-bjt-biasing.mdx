## 1. Why bias?

An amplifier must work in the **active** region for the whole signal swing. A signal is AC — it swings both ways — so we first set a steady DC operating point in the middle of the active region, the **Q-point (quiescent point)**, and let the signal ride on top of it. Setting that DC point is **biasing**.

A badly placed Q-point **clips** the signal: too close to saturation clips one side, too close to cut-off clips the other.

## 2. The DC load line

In a CE stage with collector resistor R_C, KVL around the output gives:

`V_CE = V_CC − I_C R_C`

That's a straight line on the output characteristics, the **DC load line**:

- I_C = 0 → V_CE = V_CC (cut-off end).
- V_CE = 0 → I_C = V_CC / R_C (saturation end).

The transistor's operating point must lie on this line; the base current picks **where**. Play with it:

## 3. Fixed bias — simple but unstable

One resistor R_B from V_CC to the base: `I_B = (V_CC − 0.7)/R_B`, `I_C = β I_B`.

**Worked example.** V_CC = 12 V, R_B = 470 kΩ, R_C = 2.2 kΩ, β = 100.
I_B = 11.3/470k = 24 µA; I_C = 2.4 mA; V_CE = 12 − 2.4 × 2.2 = **6.7 V** — nicely mid-line.

Now swap in a transistor with β = 200 (same part number — β varies widely): I_C = 4.8 mA, V_CE = 12 − 10.6 = **1.4 V** — almost saturated. **The Q-point depends directly on β**, which also rises with temperature. Stability factor S = 1 + β: bad.

## 4. Collector-feedback bias — better

R_B goes from the **collector** (not V_CC) to the base. If I_C rises, V_C falls, which cuts I_B and pulls I_C back. Negative feedback, moderately stable.

## 5. Voltage-divider bias — the standard

</Figure>

**How to solve it (the approximate method, valid when β·R_E ≥ 10·R2):**

1. Base voltage from the divider: `V_B = V_CC × R2/(R1 + R2)`.
2. Emitter voltage: `V_E = V_B − 0.7`.
3. Emitter current: `I_E = V_E / R_E`, and `I_C ≈ I_E`.
4. `V_CE = V_CC − I_C (R_C + R_E)`.

**Worked example.** V_CC = 12 V, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, R_C = 2.2 kΩ, R_E = 1 kΩ, β = 100.
- V_B = 12 × 10/57 = **2.11 V**
- V_E = 1.41 V → I_E = 1.41 mA ≈ I_C
- V_C = 12 − 1.41 × 2.2 = 8.9 V; V_CE = 12 − 1.41 × 3.2 = **7.5 V**

**β never appeared.** Double β to 200 and the Q-point barely moves. That's why this is the most stable and most used bias. The emitter resistor provides the feedback: if I_C rises, V_E rises, so V_BE falls, so I_B falls, which pulls I_C back.

## 6. Stability and thermal runaway

The Q-point drifts with temperature because:
1. I_CBO (leakage) doubles about every 10 °C;
2. V_BE falls about 2.5 mV per °C;
3. β rises with temperature.

**Thermal runaway:** more heat → more I_C → more power `V_CE × I_C` → more heat… until the transistor destroys itself. Prevented by stable bias (an emitter resistor), heat sinks and correct ratings.

The **stability factor** S = ∂I_C/∂I_CBO measures sensitivity: fixed bias S = 1 + β (worst); voltage-divider S is small (best).

## Lok Sewa: write it (10 marks · 16 minutes)

**Question.** What is biasing? Explain voltage-divider bias and show why it is the most stable biasing method.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Biasing** is the application of DC voltages to a transistor to set a fixed operating point (Q-point) in the active region, so that an AC signal is amplified without distortion. The Q-point must also stay stable against changes in β and temperature.

**DC load line.** From V_CE = V_CC − I_C R_C, the line joins (V_CC, 0) and (0, V_CC/R_C); the Q-point lies on it, ideally near the middle. *(Sketch output characteristics with the load line and Q.)*

**Voltage-divider bias.** R1 and R2 form a divider from V_CC fixing the base voltage; R_C is in the collector and R_E in the emitter. *(Draw.)*
- `V_B = V_CC R2/(R1 + R2)`, `V_E = V_B − V_BE`, `I_C ≈ I_E = V_E/R_E`, `V_CE = V_CC − I_C(R_C + R_E)`.

**Why it is stable.**
1. I_C depends on V_B, V_BE and R_E — **not on β** — so transistor replacement or temperature changes in β hardly move the Q-point.
2. R_E provides negative feedback: if I_C rises, V_E rises, V_BE falls, I_B falls and I_C is restored.
3. Its stability factor is small (close to 1 with a stiff divider), compared with 1 + β for fixed bias.

**Example.** V_CC = 12 V, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, R_C = 2.2 kΩ, R_E = 1 kΩ: V_B = 2.11 V, I_C ≈ 1.41 mA, V_CE ≈ 7.5 V.

**Note.** For AC, R_E is bypassed by a capacitor to keep the voltage gain high.

</details>
