## 1. Why AC exists

DC flows one way at a steady value. **AC** reverses direction regularly. The world's grids use AC for one main reason: **transformers** ([lesson 15](/learning-guide/nec/electronics/15-inductors/)) can step AC voltage up and down cheaply and efficiently. Power stations step up to very high voltage for transmission (less current, so much less I²R loss in the long lines), and substations step it down to safe levels for homes.

Nepal (like India) supplies about **230 V, 50 Hz** single-phase to homes, and 400 V three-phase to larger buildings.

## 2. How a generator makes a sine wave

Spin a coil inside a magnetic field. As it turns, the amount of field passing through it keeps changing, so by Faraday's law a voltage is induced. Work through the geometry and the voltage follows a **sine** function of the angle:

`e = Em sin θ = Em sin(ωt)`

- Coil face-on to the field → the field is changing slowest → voltage 0.
- Coil edge-on → changing fastest → voltage at its peak.
- Half a turn later the coil's sides have swapped places, so the voltage reverses.

`Em = N B A ω` — more turns, stronger field, bigger coil or faster spin → bigger voltage.

## 3. The vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Symbol / formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instantaneous value | value at one moment | v, i (lower case) |
| Cycle | one complete repetition | — |
| Period | time for one cycle | T, in seconds |
| Frequency | cycles per second | `f = 1/T`, hertz (Hz) |
| Angular frequency | radians per second | `ω = 2πf` |
| Peak (amplitude, maximum) | largest value | Vm, Im |
| Peak-to-peak | top to bottom | `Vpp = 2Vm` |
| RMS (effective) | the DC-equivalent value | `Vm/√2` (next lesson) |

**Worked example.** `v = 325 sin(314t)`.

- Peak Vm = **325 V**.
- ω = 314 rad/s → f = 314 / 2π ≈ **50 Hz**.
- Period T = 1/50 = **20 ms**.
- RMS = 325/√2 ≈ **230 V** — this is Nepal's mains, written as an equation.

**Worked example.** What's the instantaneous value 2.5 ms after a zero crossing?
ωt = 314 × 0.0025 = 0.785 rad = 45°, so v = 325 × sin 45° = 325 × 0.707 ≈ **230 V**.

## 4. Phase and phase difference

Two waves of the same frequency may not peak at the same time. The shift between them is the **phase difference**, in degrees or radians:

- `v1 = Vm sin(ωt)`
- `v2 = Vm sin(ωt − 30°)` → v2 reaches each point 30° **later**: v2 **lags** v1 by 30°.
- `v3 = Vm sin(ωt + 60°)` → v3 **leads** v1 by 60°.

**Minus means lags, plus means leads.** In circuits, the phase between voltage and current tells you what kind of load you have (lesson 19). In three-phase supplies, three voltages are 120° apart (lesson 22).

<Aside type="tip" title="Degrees vs radians">
2π rad = 360°, so π rad = 180° and π/2 = 90°. ω is always in rad/s, so convert ωt to degrees (× 180/π) before using a calculator in degree mode.
</Aside>

## Traps

- **Peak-to-peak is 2 × peak**, not peak.
- `ω = 2πf`: 314 rad/s is 50 Hz, not 314 Hz.
- "230 V mains" is the **RMS** value; the peak is about 325 V.
