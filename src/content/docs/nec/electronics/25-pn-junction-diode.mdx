## 1. Forming the junction

Join p-type and n-type silicon in one crystal:

1. Electrons from the n side **diffuse** into the p side, and holes from p into n (lesson 24, diffusion).
2. Near the junction they meet and **recombine** — they cancel each other out.
3. What's left behind is a thin layer of **fixed ions** with no free carriers: negative acceptor ions on the p side, positive donor ions on the n side. This is the **depletion region**.
4. Those ions create an electric field pointing from n to p, which pushes back against further diffusion. Equilibrium is reached quickly.
5. The voltage across the depletion region is the **barrier (built-in) potential**: about **0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for germanium**.

## 2. Forward bias: it conducts

Connect **p to +** and **n to −**.

- The external voltage opposes the barrier, so the depletion region **narrows**.
- Once the applied voltage passes the barrier — the **knee (cut-in) voltage**, ≈ 0.7 V for Si — majority carriers flood across and current **rises steeply**.
- In a working circuit a forward-biased silicon diode drops about 0.6–0.8 V almost whatever the current.

## 3. Reverse bias: it blocks

Connect **p to −** and **n to +**.

- The external voltage adds to the barrier, so the depletion region **widens**.
- Majority carriers can't cross. Only a tiny **reverse saturation current** I_s flows, carried by **minority** carriers: nanoamps for Si, microamps for Ge.
- I_s depends on temperature (roughly doubling every 10 °C), not on the reverse voltage.

## 4. The V–I characteristic and diode equation

- **Forward:** almost no current until the knee, then a steep exponential rise.
- **Reverse:** a tiny constant leakage current, until **breakdown**, where current shoots up.

The **diode (Shockley) equation**:

`I = I_s (e^(V / (η V_T)) − 1)`

- V_T = kT/q ≈ **26 mV** at room temperature (the thermal voltage).
- η (ideality factor) is 1–2.
- Forward, V ≫ 26 mV, so I grows exponentially. Reverse, the exponential vanishes and I ≈ −I_s.

**Resistances.** Static (DC) resistance `R = V/I`. **Dynamic (AC) resistance** `r_d = ΔV/ΔI ≈ η V_T / I ≈ 26 mV / I` — at 1 mA, about 26 Ω.

## 5. Breakdown

Push the reverse voltage high enough and current suddenly rises. Two mechanisms:

| | Zener breakdown | Avalanche breakdown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doping | heavy (thin depletion layer) | light |
| Voltage | low, below about 5–6 V | higher |
| Mechanism | strong field pulls electrons straight out of bonds (tunnelling) | fast carriers knock out more carriers in a chain reaction |
| Temperature coefficient | negative | positive |

Breakdown isn't destructive if current is limited — **Zener diodes** are designed to operate there as voltage references (lesson 26).

## 6. Diode models for calculations

1. **Ideal:** a perfect switch — 0 V when forward, open when reverse.
2. **Constant-voltage drop (most used):** 0.7 V when forward (0.3 V Ge), open when reverse.
3. **Piecewise linear:** 0.7 V plus a small series resistance r_d.

**Worked example.** A 5 V source, a 1 kΩ resistor and a silicon diode in series, forward biased: I = (5 − 0.7)/1,000 = **4.3 mA**. Reverse it: I ≈ 0, and the diode has all **5 V** across it.

## 7. Capacitances

- **Transition (depletion) capacitance** — reverse bias; the depletion layer acts like a capacitor's gap, so more reverse voltage → wider gap → **less** capacitance. Used on purpose in the varactor.
- **Diffusion capacitance** — forward bias; from stored charge; larger.

These capacitances limit how fast a diode can switch.

## 8. Special diodes

| Diode | Biased | What it does | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Zener** | reverse, in breakdown | holds a fixed voltage | voltage regulators, references |
| **LED** | forward | emits light as electrons and holes recombine (≈ 1.8–3.3 V drop by colour) | indicators, displays, lighting |
| **Photodiode** | reverse | light creates carriers → current proportional to light | sensors, optical receivers (fibre) |
| **Schottky** | forward | metal–semiconductor junction; ≈ 0.2–0.3 V drop, very fast | switching supplies, high-speed clamps |
| **Varactor (varicap)** | reverse | capacitance varies with voltage | electronic tuning of radios, VCOs |
| **Tunnel** | forward | very heavy doping → negative resistance region | microwave oscillators |

## Lok Sewa: write it (10 marks · 16 minutes)

**Question.** Explain the formation of a PN junction and the V–I characteristics of a PN junction diode under forward and reverse bias.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Formation.** When p-type and n-type regions are formed in one crystal, majority carriers diffuse across the junction (electrons to p, holes to n) and recombine. This leaves immobile negative acceptor ions on the p side and positive donor ions on the n side — the **depletion region**, devoid of mobile carriers. The ions set up a field (and barrier potential ≈ 0.7 V for Si, 0.3 V for Ge) that stops further diffusion. *(Draw the junction with ions and the barrier.)*

**Forward bias** (p to +, n to −). The applied voltage opposes the barrier; the depletion region narrows. Below the **cut-in voltage** the current is negligible; above it, majority carriers cross and current rises exponentially, limited only by external resistance.

**Reverse bias** (p to −, n to +). The barrier increases and the depletion region widens. Only a small, nearly constant **reverse saturation current** flows due to minority carriers (nA for Si). It depends on temperature, not voltage.

**Breakdown.** At a large reverse voltage the current rises sharply by Zener or avalanche action; without current limiting the diode may be damaged.

**Characteristic.** *(Sketch: first quadrant — flat until ≈ 0.7 V, then steep; third quadrant — small constant current, then a sharp drop at V_BR.)* Diode equation: I = I_s(e^(V/ηV_T) − 1), V_T ≈ 26 mV.

**Conclusion.** The diode conducts in one direction only, so it's used for rectification, clipping, clamping, protection and switching.

</details>
