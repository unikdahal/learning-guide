**Goal:** compute a time constant, say how charged a capacitor is after any number of time constants, and know what C and L look like at the instant of switching and long after.

<Aside type="note" title="Where this scores for you">
**NEC 1.2** (R-L and R-C circuits): "τ = ?", "after one time constant the capacitor reaches ___%". **Lok Sewa Computer Engineer 4.1.4** (transient analysis). The same curve sets the speed of every digital signal on a wire — a gate driving a long track is an RC circuit.
</Aside>

## 1. Charging a capacitor through a resistor

Connect an uncharged capacitor C through a resistor R to a battery E:

- **At the first instant** the capacitor has no voltage, so the full E sits across R. Maximum current: `E/R`.
- As charge builds, the capacitor's voltage rises, so less voltage is left across R, so the current shrinks, so charging slows down.
- It gets closer and closer to E without ever quite reaching it.

That "fast at first, then slower and slower" shape is an **exponential** curve:

`vC = E (1 − e^(−t/τ))`   and   `i = (E/R) e^(−t/τ)`

where **τ = R × C** is the **time constant**, in seconds (Ω × F = s).

<RcLab />

## 2. The numbers to memorise

| Time | Charging: vC reaches | Discharging: vC falls to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1τ | **63.2%** | **36.8%** |
| 2τ | 86.5% | 13.5% |
| 3τ | 95.0% | 5.0% |
| 4τ | 98.2% | 1.8% |
| 5τ | **99.3%** — treated as fully charged | 0.7% |

The rule of thumb: **each τ closes 63% of the remaining gap.** After **5τ** the transient is considered over.

**Worked example.** R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 µF, E = 10 V.
τ = 10,000 × 0.0001 = **1 s**. After 1 s: 6.32 V. After 2 s: 8.65 V. Effectively full after **5 s**.

**Worked example.** How long until a 47 µF capacitor through 2.2 kΩ is "fully" charged?
τ = 2,200 × 47 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 0.103 s, so 5τ ≈ **0.52 s**.

## 3. Discharging

Disconnect the battery and let the charged capacitor discharge through R: `vC = V₀ e^(−t/τ)`. After 1τ only 36.8% remains.

## 4. RL circuits — the same shape, a different τ

Switch a battery E onto a resistor R in series with an inductor L. The inductor fights the change, so the current rises gradually:

`i = (E/R)(1 − e^(−t/τ))`   with   **τ = L / R**

**Worked example.** L = 0.5 H, R = 10 Ω → τ = **0.05 s** (50 ms). The current reaches 63.2% of its final 1 A (for E = 10 V) after 50 ms.

Note the difference: RC → **multiply**; RL → **divide** (L/R). A **bigger R makes an RC circuit slower but an RL circuit faster.**

## 5. The switching-instant rules

These solve most transient MCQs without any exponentials:

| | Just after switching (t = 0⁺) | Long after (steady state) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uncharged capacitor | acts like a **short circuit** (voltage can't jump) | acts like an **open circuit** |
| Inductor with no current | acts like an **open circuit** (current can't jump) | acts like a **short circuit** |

## Traps

- τ = RC but τ = **L/R** (not LR).
- 63.2% is for **charging**; 36.8% is what **remains** when discharging.
- "Fully charged" in exam language = **5τ**.

## Lok Sewa: write it (10 marks · 16 minutes)

**Question.** Derive the expression for the voltage across a capacitor charging through a resistor from a DC source. Define time constant.

Write your own answer first, by hand and timed. Then compare.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Circuit.** A DC source E, a resistor R and an initially uncharged capacitor C in series; the switch closes at t = 0. *(Draw.)*

**Derivation.**
- KVL: `E = iR + v_C`.
- Capacitor current: `i = C dv_C/dt`.
- Substituting: `RC (dv_C/dt) + v_C = E`.
- Separating variables: `dv_C / (E − v_C) = dt / (RC)`.
- Integrating from v_C = 0 at t = 0: `−ln(E − v_C) + ln E = t/RC`.
- Therefore **`v_C = E (1 − e^(−t/RC))`** and **`i = (E/R) e^(−t/RC)`**.

**Time constant.** τ = RC (seconds). It is the time in which the capacitor voltage would reach its final value if it kept rising at its initial rate; equivalently, the time to reach **63.2%** of the final value (since 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.632). The current falls to 36.8% of its initial value in one τ.

**Practical completion.** After 5τ the capacitor reaches 99.3% and the transient is considered over.

**RL dual.** For an inductor switched onto E through R: `i = (E/R)(1 − e^(−tR/L))`, with τ = L/R.

**Example.** R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 µF, E = 10 V: τ = 1 s; v_C(2 s) = 10(1 − e⁻²) = 8.65 V.

**Applications.** Timing circuits, delay elements, smoothing, and the rise time of digital signals on interconnects.

</details>
