**Goal:** know what RMS means physically, never confuse the full-cycle and half-cycle average, and recall form and peak factors for the common waveforms.

<Aside type="note" title="Where this scores for you">

</Aside>

## 1. The problem

A sine wave spends equal time positive and negative. So what single number describes "how strong" 230 V AC is?

## 2. Average value — two different things

- **Over a full cycle:** the positive half cancels the negative half, so the average is **zero**. Correct, but useless for describing strength.
- **Over a half cycle** (the usual "average value" in textbooks): `Vavg = 2Vm/π ≈ 0.637 Vm`.

<Aside type="caution" title="Read the question carefully">
Practice question banks are sloppy here: some say "the average value of a sine is zero", others "2Vm/π", and a few mark one as wrong. Both are right for their interval. If the question says **complete/full cycle → 0**. If it just says "average value" or "half cycle" → **0.637 Vm**.
</Aside>

## 3. RMS — the value that means something

**RMS (root mean square)** is defined by heating:

> The RMS value of an AC current is the **steady DC current that would produce the same heat** in the same resistor over the same time.

That's why it's also called the **effective value**, and why a 230 V RMS kettle boils water exactly as fast as it would on 230 V DC.

**Where `Vm/√2` comes from:** heating goes with `v²`. Square a sine wave and you get a wave that swings between 0 and Vm², averaging exactly half: **mean of sin² = ½**. So the mean of v² is Vm²/2, and its square root is:

`Vrms = Vm / √2 ≈ 0.707 Vm`  and  `Vm = √2 Vrms ≈ 1.414 Vrms`

- Mains: 230 V RMS → 325 V peak → 650 V peak-to-peak.
- Ammeters and voltmeters for AC read **RMS** unless they say otherwise.

## 4. Form factor and peak factor

| Name | Definition | Sine value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Form factor** | RMS ÷ average (half-cycle) | π/(2√2) ≈ **1.11** |
| **Peak (crest) factor** | peak ÷ RMS | √2 ≈ **1.414** |

Rearranged (a favourite MCQ): **peak = RMS × peak factor**, **average = RMS ÷ form factor**.

## 5. Other waveforms

| Waveform | RMS | Average (half cycle) | Form factor | Peak factor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sine | Vm/√2 | 2Vm/π | 1.11 | 1.414 |
| Square | Vm | Vm | **1** | **1** |
| Triangle | Vm/√3 | Vm/2 | 1.155 | 1.732 |
| Half-wave rectified sine | Vm/2 | Vm/π (full cycle) | 1.57 | 2 |
| Full-wave rectified sine | Vm/√2 | 2Vm/π | 1.11 | 1.414 |

A **square wave** has the same value all the time apart from its sign, so RMS = average = peak.

## 6. Worked examples

- A sine current has peak 14.14 A. RMS = 14.14 × 0.707 = **10 A**; half-cycle average = 14.14 × 0.637 = **9 A**; check form factor 10/9 = 1.11 ✔.
- A heater on 230 V RMS with R = 46 Ω: I = 5 A RMS, P = 230 × 5 = **1,150 W** — use RMS values in power formulas.
- A voltmeter reads 120 V on a sine supply. Peak = 120 × 1.414 = **170 V**.

## Traps

- Full-cycle average 0 vs half-cycle average 0.637 Vm (above).
- **Use RMS** in `P = VI`, not peak.
- Form factor is RMS/**average**; peak factor is **peak**/RMS.

## Lok Sewa: write it (5 marks · 8 minutes)

**Question.** Derive the RMS and average values of a sinusoidal voltage and find its form factor and peak factor.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Let v = Vm sin θ, θ = ωt. *(Sketch one cycle and v².)*

**RMS value** — the DC value that would produce the same heating in a resistor:
V²rms = (1/2π) ∫₀^2π Vm² sin²θ dθ = (Vm²/2π) ∫₀^2π (1 − cos 2θ)/2 dθ = (Vm²/2π)(π) = Vm²/2
→ **Vrms = Vm/√2 ≈ 0.707 Vm**.

**Average value** — over a full cycle the positive and negative halves cancel, so the average is **0**; the conventional average is taken over a **half cycle**:
Vavg = (1/π) ∫₀^π Vm sin θ dθ = (Vm/π)[−cos θ]₀^π = (Vm/π)(2) → **Vavg = 2Vm/π ≈ 0.637 Vm**.

**Form factor** = Vrms / Vavg = (Vm/√2)/(2Vm/π) = π/(2√2) ≈ **1.11**.

**Peak (crest) factor** = Vm / Vrms = **√2 ≈ 1.414**.

**Significance:** RMS is what meters and ratings use (220 V mains has Vm ≈ 311 V); the form factor indicates the waveform's shape (square wave 1.0, triangle 1.155).

</details>
