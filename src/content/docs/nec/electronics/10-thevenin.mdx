**Goal:** replace any linear network, as seen from two terminals, by a single voltage source Vth in series with a single resistor Rth — and use it to find a load current in seconds.

<Aside type="note" title="Where this scores for you">
**NEC 1.2** — the model paper asks what the Thevenin voltage means. **Lok Sewa Computer Engineer unit 4.1.1** — "State Thevenin's theorem and find the current in RL" is a classic 5- or 10-mark Paper II question; the answer template is at the bottom.
</Aside>

## The idea

Stand at two terminals, a and b, and look into a circuit full of sources and resistors. Whatever's inside, **from the outside it behaves exactly like one battery with one resistor in series**. Anything you connect across a–b (the **load**) can't tell the difference.

> Any linear, bilateral two-terminal network can be replaced by an equivalent circuit of a voltage source **Vth** in series with a resistance **Rth**.

- **Vth** = the **open-circuit voltage** across a–b (nothing connected).
- **Rth** = the resistance seen **looking into** a–b with every independent source switched off (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened).

Why bother? If the load changes — try 2 Ω, then 6 Ω, then 10 Ω — you'd otherwise re-solve the whole circuit each time. With Thevenin you solve it **once**, then each load is just `I_L = Vth / (Rth + RL)`.

## Worked example

<Figure caption="Left: a 12 V source with 4 Ω in series and 12 Ω across the terminals a–b. Right: its Thevenin equivalent.">
  <Circuit name="thevenin" label="Left: a 12 volt battery, a 4 ohm resistor along the top wire, and a 12 ohm resistor across the output terminals a and b. Right: the equivalent — a 9 volt source in series with 3 ohms, feeding the same terminals a and b." />
</Figure>

**Step 1 — Vth (open-circuit voltage).** With nothing across a–b, the 4 Ω and 12 Ω are in series across 12 V. Vth is the voltage across the 12 Ω (voltage divider):

`Vth = 12 × 12/(4 + 12) = 9 V`

**Step 2 — Rth.** Short the 12 V source. Looking in from a–b you see 4 Ω and 12 Ω in parallel:

`Rth = 4 × 12/(4 + 12) = 3 Ω`

**Step 3 — use it.** Connect a load RL = 6 Ω:

`I_L = 9/(3 + 6) = 1 A`

**Check the long way.** 12 ∥ 6 = 4 Ω; total 4 + 4 = 8 Ω; supply current 12/8 = 1.5 A; voltage across a–b = 1.5 × 4 = 6 V; load current 6/6 = 1 A ✔.

Now other loads cost one line each:

| RL | I_L = 9/(3 + RL) | V_L = I_L × RL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Ω | 2.25 A | 2.25 V |
| 3 Ω | 1.5 A | 4.5 V |
| 6 Ω | 1 A | 6 V |
| 15 Ω | 0.5 A | 7.5 V |
| open (∞) | 0 | 9 V = Vth |

The last row shows what Vth *is*: the voltage you'd measure with nothing connected.

## A way to measure it on a real circuit

- Measure the open-circuit voltage with a voltmeter → Vth.
- Measure the short-circuit current with an ammeter (carefully) → Isc.
- Then `Rth = Vth / Isc`. (Here: Isc = 12/4 = 3 A, and 9/3 = 3 Ω ✔.) This link to Norton is the next lesson.

## Traps

- Rth is found with sources **off**, Vth with sources **on**. Swapping these is the classic mistake.
- Remove the load before finding Vth — it's an **open-circuit** voltage.
- Only for linear networks; the load itself may be anything.

**Question.** State Thevenin's theorem. Using it, find the current through a 6 Ω load connected across a–b of a network with a 12 V source, 4 Ω series resistance and 12 Ω across a–b.

Write your own answer first, by hand and timed. Then compare.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Statement.** Any linear, bilateral, two-terminal network can be replaced by an equivalent circuit consisting of a single voltage source V_th in series with a single resistance R_th, where V_th is the open-circuit voltage across the terminals and R_th is the resistance seen from the terminals with all independent sources replaced by their internal resistances.

**Diagram.** Original network → V_th in series with R_th → load R_L across a–b. *(Draw both.)*

**Procedure.**
1. Remove the load R_L and mark terminals a–b.
2. Find V_th: the open-circuit voltage across a–b.
3. Find R_th: short voltage sources, open current sources, and compute the resistance seen from a–b.
4. Draw the Thevenin equivalent, reconnect R_L: `I_L = V_th / (R_th + R_L)`.

**Solution of the example.**
- V_th = 12 × 12/(4 + 12) = 9 V.
- R_th = 4 ∥ 12 = (4 × 12)/16 = 3 Ω.
- I_L = 9/(3 + 6) = **1 A**. Check by direct method: 12 ∥ 6 = 4 Ω; total 8 Ω; supply 1.5 A; V_ab = 6 V; I_L = 6/6 = 1 A ✔.

**Advantages.** The network is solved once; any number of load values can then be tried in one line. It also gives the load for maximum power transfer (R_L = R_th).

**Limitations.** Only for linear networks; the equivalent is valid only at the terminals, not for power inside the network.

**Conclusion.** Thevenin's theorem reduces a complex linear network to its simplest form as seen by a load; its dual is Norton's theorem (I_N = V_th/R_th, R_N = R_th).

</details>

## Check yourself

1. The Thevenin voltage is:
   (a) the short-circuit voltage at the terminals (b) the open-circuit voltage at the terminals (c) the source's EMF (d) the voltage across Rth

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) the voltage across a–b with the load removed.</details>

2. To find Rth, the independent voltage sources are:
   (a) open-circuited (b) short-circuited (c) left as they are (d) doubled

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) replaced by short circuits.</details>

3. A network has Vth = 10 V and Rth = 5 Ω. The current in a 15 Ω load is:
   (a) 2 A (b) 0.5 A (c) 0.67 A (d) 1 A

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) 10/(5 + 15) = 0.5 A.</details>

4. 24 V in series with 6 Ω, with 6 Ω across the output terminals. Vth and Rth are:
   (a) 24 V, 12 Ω (b) 12 V, 3 Ω (c) 12 V, 6 Ω (d) 24 V, 3 Ω

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) Vth = 24 × 6/12 = 12 V; Rth = 6 ∥ 6 = 3 Ω.</details>

5. Thevenin's equivalent circuit consists of:
   (a) a current source in parallel with a resistor (b) a voltage source in series with a resistor (c) a voltage source in parallel with a resistor (d) a current source in series with a resistor

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) Norton's is (a).</details>
