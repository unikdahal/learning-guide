## 1. Conductors, insulators and the middle ground

Recall [lesson 3](/learning-guide/nec/electronics/03-resistance-ohm-power/): metals have many free electrons, insulators almost none. Physics explains the difference with **energy bands**:

- Electrons bound in atoms sit in the **valence band**.
- To conduct, an electron must jump to the **conduction band**.
- Between them lies a **forbidden gap** (band gap, E_g), measured in electron-volts (eV).

| Material | Band gap | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conductor (copper) | bands overlap — no gap | always conducts |
| Semiconductor | **silicon ≈ 1.1 eV, germanium ≈ 0.7 eV** | a few electrons jump at room temperature |
| Insulator (diamond, glass) | large, ≈ 5 eV or more | essentially none jump |

## 2. Pure (intrinsic) silicon

Silicon and germanium are **tetravalent**: each atom has 4 valence electrons, shared in **covalent bonds** with 4 neighbours to form a crystal.

- At absolute zero, every electron is locked in a bond: silicon is an insulator.
- At room temperature, heat breaks a few bonds. Each freed electron leaves behind an empty spot, a **hole**.
- A hole behaves like a **positive charge**: a neighbouring electron can hop into it, which moves the hole the other way.
- So a semiconductor conducts by **two** carriers: free electrons (negative) and holes (positive). In pure silicon they come in equal numbers: `n = p = nᵢ` (the intrinsic concentration).
- **Heat more → more bonds break → more carriers → resistance falls.** Semiconductors have a **negative temperature coefficient** of resistance, opposite to metals.

## 3. Doping: making it useful

Pure silicon conducts too little to be useful. Adding a tiny amount of impurity — **doping**, around 1 atom per million or less — changes everything.

### n-type: add a pentavalent impurity

Add atoms with **5** valence electrons: **phosphorus, arsenic, antimony**. Four electrons bond with silicon neighbours; the fifth is barely held and becomes **free**.

- These impurities **donate** electrons → called **donors**.
- **Majority carriers: electrons. Minority carriers: holes.**
- Each donor that gives up its electron becomes a fixed **positive ion** in the crystal.

### p-type: add a trivalent impurity

Add atoms with **3** valence electrons: **boron, aluminium, gallium, indium**. One bond is left incomplete — a **hole**.

- These impurities **accept** electrons into their holes → called **acceptors**.
- **Majority carriers: holes. Minority carriers: electrons.**
- Each acceptor that has taken an electron becomes a fixed **negative ion**.

<Aside type="caution" title="Classic trap">
**n-type and p-type material are both electrically neutral overall.** "n" and "p" name the majority **carrier**, not a net charge: every free electron in n-type is balanced by a positive donor ion.
</Aside>

**Mass-action law.** In any doped semiconductor at equilibrium, `n × p = nᵢ²`. Adding donors raises n and pushes p down by the same factor.

## 4. How carriers move

- **Drift:** an electric field pushes carriers — electrons against the field, holes with it. This is ordinary conduction.
- **Diffusion:** carriers spread from where they're crowded to where they're sparse, with no field needed — like a drop of ink in water. Diffusion is what makes the PN junction work (next lesson).

## 5. Silicon vs germanium

| | Silicon | Germanium |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Band gap | ≈ 1.1 eV | ≈ 0.7 eV |
| Diode knee voltage | ≈ 0.7 V | ≈ 0.3 V |
| Leakage (reverse) current | nA — very small | µA — larger |
| Maximum temperature | ≈ 150–200 °C | ≈ 75–100 °C |
| Availability | sand — abundant, cheap | rare |

Silicon wins for almost everything; that's why it's "Silicon Valley".

## Lok Sewa: write it (5 marks · 8 minutes)

**Question.** Distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors. Explain n-type and p-type semiconductors.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Intrinsic semiconductor.** A pure semiconductor (Si, Ge). Carriers are produced only by thermal breaking of covalent bonds, so the number of free electrons equals the number of holes (n = p = nᵢ). Conductivity is low and strongly temperature-dependent.

**Extrinsic semiconductor.** A semiconductor doped with a small amount of impurity to increase conductivity; carrier concentrations are set mainly by the doping.

**n-type.** Doped with a pentavalent (donor) impurity — P, As, Sb. Four of its valence electrons form covalent bonds; the fifth becomes free. Electrons are majority carriers, holes minority; donor atoms become immobile positive ions. *(Sketch the lattice with the extra electron.)*

**p-type.** Doped with a trivalent (acceptor) impurity — B, Al, Ga, In. One bond is incomplete, creating a hole. Holes are majority carriers, electrons minority; acceptor atoms become immobile negative ions.

Both types remain electrically neutral. In both, n·p = nᵢ² at equilibrium.

</details>
