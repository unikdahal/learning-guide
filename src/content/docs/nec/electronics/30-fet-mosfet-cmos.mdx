## 1. The field-effect idea

A BJT is **current-controlled** (base current controls collector current). A **field-effect transistor (FET)** is **voltage-controlled**: the voltage on a **gate** creates an electric field that widens or narrows a conducting **channel** between **source** and **drain**. Almost no gate current flows, so the input impedance is **very high**.

FETs are **unipolar** — only majority carriers carry current (electrons in n-channel, holes in p-channel). BJTs are **bipolar** — both carriers matter.

## 2. JFET (briefly)

A bar of n-type silicon (the channel) with p-type gate regions on its sides. The gate–channel junction is kept **reverse-biased**; more negative V_GS widens the depletion regions and squeezes the channel.

- At V_GS = 0, maximum current **I_DSS** flows.
- At V_GS = V_P (the **pinch-off** voltage, negative for n-channel), the channel closes: I_D = 0.
- Shockley's equation: `I_D = I_DSS (1 − V_GS/V_P)²`.

## 3. MOSFET

**M**etal–**O**xide–**S**emiconductor FET: the gate is a conductor separated from the channel by a very thin layer of silicon dioxide, an **insulator**. Input impedance is enormous (≈ 10¹²–10¹⁵ Ω).

**Enhancement MOSFET (n-channel)** — the kind in every chip:
- **Normally OFF**: no channel exists at V_GS = 0.
- Make V_GS positive beyond the **threshold voltage V_T** (≈ 0.3–1 V in modern chips), and electrons are drawn under the gate to form an n-channel: it turns ON.

**Depletion MOSFET:** a channel is built in, so it's **normally ON**; V_GS can enhance or deplete it.

**Regions (n-channel enhancement):**

| Region | Condition | Behaviour | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cut-off | V_GS < V_T | I_D ≈ 0 | switch OFF |
| Ohmic / triode | V_GS > V_T, small V_DS | acts like a voltage-controlled resistor | switch ON |
| Saturation | V_GS > V_T, V_DS ≥ V_GS − V_T | `I_D = k (V_GS − V_T)²`, nearly independent of V_DS | amplifier |

**Transconductance** `g_m = ΔI_D/ΔV_GS = 2k(V_GS − V_T)` — the FET's "gain" (in siemens).

**Worked example.** k = 0.5 mA/V², V_T = 1 V, V_GS = 3 V: I_D = 0.5 × (3 − 1)² = **2 mA**; g_m = 2 × 0.5 × 2 = **2 mA/V**.

<Aside type="caution" title="Handle with care">
The gate oxide is only nanometres thick; static electricity from a finger can punch through it. That's why chips ship in anti-static bags and have protection diodes on their pins.
</Aside>

## 4. BJT vs FET

| | BJT | FET / MOSFET |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Controlled by | base **current** | gate **voltage** |
| Carriers | both (bipolar) | majority only (unipolar) |
| Input impedance | low–medium | very high |
| Gain | higher voltage gain per stage | lower, but improving |
| Noise | more | less |
| Thermal stability | thermal runaway possible | better (current falls as it heats) |
| Size, density | larger | very small — billions per chip |
| Typical use | discrete analog, RF | digital ICs, power switching |

## 5. CMOS — complementary MOS

Pair an n-channel MOSFET (pull-down, to ground) with a p-channel MOSFET (pull-up, to V_DD), gates tied together. This is the **CMOS inverter**, the basic cell of digital logic:

- **Input HIGH →** nMOS ON, pMOS OFF → output pulled to ground → **LOW**.
- **Input LOW →** pMOS ON, nMOS OFF → output pulled to V_DD → **HIGH**.

**In either steady state, one transistor is OFF**, so there's no path from V_DD to ground: **static power ≈ 0**. Power is used mainly while **switching**, charging and discharging the load capacitance:

`P_dynamic ≈ C V_DD² f`

That one formula explains a lot you've seen at work: CPUs get hotter at higher clock frequency f, and lowering supply voltage V_DD saves power quadratically — the reason for DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling).

**Why CMOS dominates:** near-zero static power, high noise margin (output swings rail to rail), huge fan-out (gates draw no DC current), and tiny transistors that pack densely. NAND and NOR gates are built by combining nMOS and pMOS in series/parallel.

## Lok Sewa: write it (10 marks · 16 minutes)

**Question.** Explain the construction and working of an n-channel enhancement MOSFET. Describe the CMOS inverter and its advantages.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Construction.** A lightly doped p-type substrate with two heavily doped n⁺ regions — source and drain. A thin SiO₂ layer covers the region between them, and a metal (or polysilicon) gate sits on the oxide. The substrate (body) is usually tied to the source. *(Draw the cross-section and symbol.)*

**Working.** With V_GS = 0 there is no channel; the drain–source path is two back-to-back PN junctions, so I_D ≈ 0 (normally off). When V_GS is made positive, the gate field repels holes and attracts electrons beneath the oxide. Above the threshold V_T an n-type **inversion layer** forms, linking source and drain. Applying V_DS drives drain current. Increasing V_GS enhances the channel and raises I_D.
- Ohmic region (small V_DS): channel acts as a resistor controlled by V_GS.
- Saturation (V_DS ≥ V_GS − V_T): channel pinches off at the drain end; `I_D = k(V_GS − V_T)²`.
*(Sketch drain characteristics and the transfer curve.)*

**CMOS inverter.** A pMOS (source to V_DD) and an nMOS (source to ground) with common gates (input) and common drains (output). Input high: nMOS on, pMOS off, output low. Input low: pMOS on, nMOS off, output high. *(Draw.)*

**Advantages of CMOS.** Negligible static power dissipation; rail-to-rail output and high noise margin; very high input impedance and large fan-out; high packing density; operates over a wide supply range. Dynamic power P ≈ CV²f.

</details>
