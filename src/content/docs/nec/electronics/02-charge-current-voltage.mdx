**Goal:** explain in plain words what charge, current and voltage are, and use `I = Q/t` and `V = W/Q`.

## 1. Charge — the thing that moves

Everything is made of atoms. An atom has a centre (nucleus) with **protons**, which carry positive charge, surrounded by **electrons**, which carry negative charge. Opposite charges attract; like charges repel.

**Charge** (symbol Q) is measured in **coulombs** (C). One electron carries a tiny charge: about 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. So one coulomb is the charge of about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons — a useful fact for MCQs.

In a metal wire, some electrons in each atom are only loosely held. These **free electrons** wander randomly through the metal. Electricity in a wire is these free electrons moving together in one direction.

## 2. Current — how fast charge flows

**Current** (I) is the amount of charge passing a point each second:

`I = Q / t`  (amperes = coulombs per second)

One **ampere** (A) means one coulomb passes every second.

**Worked example.** 30 C of charge passes through a wire in 1 minute. Current?
Convert time first: 1 minute = 60 s. `I = 30 / 60 = 0.5 A`.

**Worked example.** A phone charger supplies 2 A for 1 hour. How much charge?
`Q = I × t = 2 × 3,600 = 7,200 C`.

### Conventional current vs electron flow

Early scientists guessed that positive charge flows, from the **+ terminal to the − terminal**. We now know that in metals it's electrons that move, from − to +. Nobody rewrote the textbooks, so:

- **Conventional current** (what every circuit diagram and formula uses) flows **+ → −** outside the source.
- **Electron flow** is the opposite direction.

Both describe the same thing; the maths gives identical answers. **Always use conventional current** unless a question says "electron flow".

<Aside type="note" title="Electrons are slow, the signal is fast">
Individual electrons drift through a wire at well under a millimetre per second. A lamp still lights instantly because the wire is already full of free electrons: pushing at one end moves all of them at once, like pushing a tube already full of marbles.
</Aside>

## 3. Voltage — the push

Charge doesn't flow on its own. Something must give it energy. **Voltage** (V) is the **energy given to (or used by) each coulomb of charge**:

`V = W / Q`  (volts = joules per coulomb)

One **volt** means each coulomb carries one joule of energy. A 9 V battery gives every coulomb 9 J.

- **Voltage is always between two points.** "The voltage at point A" really means "between A and a chosen reference point", usually called **ground** or 0 V.
- That's why it's also called **potential difference (p.d.)**.
- **EMF (electromotive force)** is the voltage a *source* (battery, generator) creates — energy supplied per coulomb. **Potential difference** across a component is energy *used* per coulomb there. Both are measured in volts.

**Worked example.** A battery moves 5 C through a lamp and the lamp converts 60 J of energy. Voltage across the lamp?
`V = 60 / 5 = 12 V`.

### The water analogy, and where it breaks

| Electricity | Water in pipes |
| --- | --- |
| Voltage | water pressure difference (pump) |
| Current | flow rate (litres per second) |
| Resistance (next lesson) | a narrow section of pipe |

Useful for intuition, but don't push it far: wires are never "empty" and charge doesn't leak out of an open wire.

## 4. A complete circuit

Current flows only around a **closed loop**: source → wire → component → wire → back to the source. Break the loop anywhere (a switch, a cut wire) and current stops everywhere in that loop. That's an **open circuit**.

A path with almost zero resistance connecting the two terminals of a source is a **short circuit** — huge current, heat, often fire. Fuses and circuit breakers exist to stop it.

## 5. DC and AC

- **DC (direct current):** flows in one direction at a steady value. Batteries, phone chargers' outputs, solar panels.
- **AC (alternating current):** reverses direction many times a second. Mains electricity in Nepal and India: about 230 V, 50 Hz (50 complete reversals per second). AC has its own lessons later.

## How to measure

- An **ammeter** measures current. It goes **in series** (the current must pass through it), so it has very **low** resistance.
- A **voltmeter** measures voltage. It goes **in parallel** (across the two points), so it has very **high** resistance.

## Traps

- "Voltage flows through a resistor" — wrong. Current flows *through*; voltage is *across*.
- Energy per unit charge = **voltage**. Charge per unit time = **current**. Energy per unit time = **power** (next lesson).
- Forgetting to convert minutes and hours to seconds in `I = Q/t`.
