**Goal:** read any value like 4.7 kΩ, 250 mA or 10 µF instantly, and convert it to plain units before calculating.

## Why this comes first

Electronics values span a huge range: a phone draws thousandths of an ampere, a power line carries thousands. Engineers write these with **prefixes** instead of long strings of zeros. Most beginner mistakes in numericals are not physics mistakes — they're prefix mistakes.

## The quantities in Chapter 1

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Unit symbol |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Charge | Q | coulomb | C |
| Current | I | ampere | A |
| Voltage (potential difference) | V | volt | V |
| Resistance | R | ohm | Ω |
| Conductance | G | siemens (older name: mho) | S |
| Power | P | watt | W |
| Energy | W or E | joule | J |
| Time | t | second | s |
| Capacitance | C | farad | F |
| Inductance | L | henry | H |
| Frequency | f | hertz | Hz |

You'll meet each of these properly in its own lesson. For now just notice: the **symbol** of the quantity (italic V for voltage) and the **unit** (V for volt) can look the same. Read from context.

## Prefixes

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiply by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| giga | G | 10⁹ (1,000,000,000) | 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi |
| mega | M | 10⁶ (1,000,000) | 1 MΩ resistor |
| kilo | k | 10³ (1,000) | 4.7 kΩ = 4,700 Ω |
| — | — | 1 | — |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ (0.001) | 250 mA = 0.25 A |
| micro | µ (often written u) | 10⁻⁶ (0.000001) | 10 µF = 0.00001 F |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ | 100 nF = 0.1 µF |
| pico | p | 10⁻¹² | 22 pF |

**Capital M is mega, small m is milli.** 1 MΩ is a billion times bigger than 1 mΩ.

## How to convert

Two rules:

1. **Before calculating, convert everything to plain units** (A, V, Ω, s).
2. **Moving to a smaller unit makes the number bigger**, and vice versa. 1 A = 1,000 mA; 1 mA = 0.001 A.

**Worked example.** A 2.2 kΩ resistor carries 5 mA. Write both in plain units.

- 2.2 kΩ = 2.2 × 1,000 = 2,200 Ω
- 5 mA = 5 × 0.001 = 0.005 A

**Worked example.** Express 0.000047 F with a prefix.

- 0.000047 = 47 × 10⁻⁶, so 47 µF.

<Aside type="tip" title="Shortcut">
kΩ × mA = V directly: 2.2 kΩ × 5 mA = 11 V, because 10³ × 10⁻³ = 1. You'll use this constantly in transistor circuits.
</Aside>

## Traps

- Reading "m" as mega.
- Forgetting that 1 kWh is energy, not power (lesson 3).
- Mixing minutes and seconds: SI time is in **seconds**, but electricity bills use **hours**.

## Check yourself

1. 4.7 kΩ in ohms is:
   (a) 0.0047 Ω (b) 47 Ω (c) 4,700 Ω (d) 47,000 Ω

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(c) 4,700 Ω — kilo means × 1,000.</details>

2. 350 mA in amperes is:
   (a) 0.35 A (b) 3.5 A (c) 35 A (d) 350,000 A

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) 0.35 A — milli means ÷ 1,000.</details>

3. Which is the largest? (a) 1 MΩ (b) 900 kΩ (c) 1,000 Ω (d) 1 mΩ

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) 1 MΩ = 1,000,000 Ω; 900 kΩ = 900,000 Ω.</details>

4. 0.1 µF equals:
   (a) 1 nF (b) 10 nF (c) 100 nF (d) 100 pF

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(c) 100 nF, since 1 µF = 1,000 nF.</details>

5. The unit of conductance is:
   (a) ohm (b) siemens (c) henry (d) farad

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(b) siemens (also called mho, "ohm" spelled backwards).</details>
