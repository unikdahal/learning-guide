## Why we need them

Series and parallel rules only work when a network *can* be split into series and parallel groups. Put two batteries in different branches and those rules stop working. Kirchhoff's two laws work on **any** circuit.

First, three words:

- **Node:** a point where two or more components connect (a junction).
- **Branch:** a path between two nodes containing one or more components.
- **Loop:** any closed path; a **mesh** is a loop with no other loop inside it.

## Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)

> At any node, the total current flowing **in** equals the total current flowing **out**. Equivalently, the algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero.

KCL is **conservation of charge**: charge can't pile up at a junction or vanish there.

## Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)

> Around any closed loop, the algebraic sum of all voltages is zero. Equivalently, the total EMF (rises) equals the total voltage drops.

KVL is **conservation of energy**: a coulomb that goes all the way around a loop and returns to its start point ends with the same energy it began with.

**Sign convention** (pick one and stick to it): walk around the loop. Going through a source from − to + is a **rise** (+V). Going through a resistor *in the direction of the current* is a **drop** (−IR).

**Quick example.** A 12 V battery drives current through 2 Ω and 4 Ω in series. KVL: `12 − 2I − 4I = 0` → `I = 2 A`. That's exactly what the series rule gave — KVL is where that rule comes from.

## Solving a real circuit: nodal analysis (KCL)

<Figure caption="Two sources that can't be reduced with series–parallel rules. Unknown: the voltage V at the top middle node.">
  <Circuit name="nodal" label="A 10 V source on the left feeds a 2 ohm resistor to a middle node V; a 4 ohm resistor runs from that node down to the 0 V bottom wire; another 4 ohm resistor runs from the node to a 6 V source on the right. Both sources have + on top." />
</Figure>

**Step 1 — choose a reference.** The bottom wire is 0 V. The only unknown is the node voltage **V**.

**Step 2 — write each branch current with Ohm's law**, flowing *into* the node:

- From the 10 V source through 2 Ω: `(10 − V) / 2`
- From the 6 V source through 4 Ω: `(6 − V) / 4`
- Out of the node, down through 4 Ω: `V / 4`

**Step 3 — KCL: in = out.**

`(10 − V)/2 + (6 − V)/4 = V/4`

Multiply every term by 4: `2(10 − V) + (6 − V) = V` → `20 − 2V + 6 − V = V` → `26 = 4V` → **V = 6.5 V**.

**Step 4 — find the currents.**

- Left branch: (10 − 6.5)/2 = **1.75 A** into the node.
- Right branch: (6 − 6.5)/4 = **−0.125 A**. The minus sign means our guess was wrong: 0.125 A actually flows **out** of the node, into the 6 V source (it's being charged).
- Middle: 6.5/4 = **1.625 A** down.
- Check with KCL: 1.75 − 0.125 = 1.625. ✔

<Aside type="tip" title="Negative answers aren't mistakes">
Guess any direction for an unknown current. If the answer comes out negative, the real current flows the other way, with the same size.
</Aside>

## The other method: mesh analysis (KVL)

Same circuit, solved a second way. Give each window (**mesh**) its own circulating current, both clockwise: **I₁** in the left mesh, **I₂** in the right. The middle 4 Ω belongs to both meshes, so it carries **I₁ − I₂** (downwards).

**Left mesh, clockwise from the bottom-left corner:**
- Up through the 10 V source, − to +: **+10**.
- Through the 2 Ω, with I₁: **−2I₁**.
- Down through the middle 4 Ω, with (I₁ − I₂): **−4(I₁ − I₂)**.
- KVL: `10 − 2I₁ − 4(I₁ − I₂) = 0` → **`6I₁ − 4I₂ = 10`**.

**Right mesh, clockwise from the middle node:**
- Through the top-right 4 Ω, with I₂: **−4I₂**.
- Down through the 6 V source, + to −: **−6**.
- Up through the middle 4 Ω, with (I₂ − I₁): **−4(I₂ − I₁)**.
- KVL: `−4I₂ − 6 − 4(I₂ − I₁) = 0` → **`4I₁ − 8I₂ = 6`**.

**Solve.** Double the first equation: 12I₁ − 8I₂ = 20. Subtract the second: 8I₁ = 14 → **I₁ = 1.75 A**. Then 8I₂ = 4(1.75) − 6 = 1 → **I₂ = 0.125 A**.

**Read the answers.**
- Left source current = I₁ = 1.75 A ✔ (nodal analysis gave the same).
- Middle resistor: I₁ − I₂ = **1.625 A** downwards ✔.
- I₂ is positive and clockwise, so 0.125 A flows left-to-right along the top-right resistor and **into** the 6 V source's + terminal — charging it ✔.

**Which to use?** Count unknowns: nodal needs one equation per node (minus the reference); mesh needs one per mesh. Here nodal needed 1 equation and mesh needed 2, so nodal was quicker. For MCQs remember: **nodal → KCL**, **mesh → KVL**. Mesh analysis works only for **planar** circuits (drawable without crossing wires).

## Traps

- KCL ↔ charge; KVL ↔ energy. Swapping them is the single most common MCQ error.
- Kirchhoff's laws apply to **any** circuit — linear or non-linear, DC or AC (with instantaneous or phasor values).
- Mixing sign conventions halfway round a loop.

<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Introduction.** Ohm's law relates voltage and current in one element. Kirchhoff's two laws (1845) describe how elements behave when connected into a network. Together they can solve any lumped electrical circuit.

**1. Kirchhoff's current law (KCL).**
*Statement:* the algebraic sum of currents meeting at a node is zero; that is, total current entering a node equals total current leaving it: `ΣI_in = ΣI_out`.
*Basis:* conservation of charge — charge cannot be created, destroyed or stored at a junction.
*Example:* at a node, I₁ = 3 A and I₂ = 2 A enter; the only leaving branch must carry I₃ = 5 A. *(Draw the node with three arrows.)*

**2. Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL).**
*Statement:* the algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop is zero; that is, the sum of EMFs equals the sum of voltage drops: `ΣE = ΣIR`.
*Basis:* conservation of energy — a charge returning to its starting point has gained and lost equal energy.
*Sign convention:* a source traversed from − to + is a rise (+); a resistor traversed in the direction of current is a drop (−IR).
*Example:* a 12 V battery with 2 Ω and 4 Ω in series: 12 − 2I − 4I = 0 → I = 2 A; drops are 4 V and 8 V, which add to 12 V. *(Draw the loop.)*

**3. Applications.** KCL is the basis of **nodal analysis**; KVL is the basis of **mesh (loop) analysis**. Both are used to solve circuits with several sources that cannot be reduced by series–parallel rules.

**4. Limitations.** Valid for lumped circuits; at very high frequencies (long lines, antennas) distributed effects need field theory.

**Conclusion.** KCL and KVL, with Ohm's law, are the foundation of all circuit analysis methods and network theorems.

</details>
