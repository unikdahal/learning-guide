## 1. The idea

Take a fraction β of the output and feed it back to the input:

- **Negative feedback:** the fed-back signal **opposes** the input. The amplifier corrects its own errors.
- **Positive feedback:** it **adds** to the input. Gain grows, and at the limit the circuit oscillates (lesson 34).

Engineering systems everywhere use negative feedback: a thermostat, cruise control, the body's temperature regulation — and every op-amp circuit.

## 2. The gain formula

With open-loop gain A and feedback fraction β:

`A_f = A / (1 + Aβ)`

- `Aβ` is the **loop gain**; `1 + Aβ` is the **amount of feedback** (often in dB).
- If `Aβ ≫ 1`, then **`A_f ≈ 1/β`** — the gain depends only on the feedback network (usually two resistors), **not on the transistor**.

**Worked example.** A = 1,000, β = 0.1: A_f = 1,000/101 = **9.90**. Now A drops by half to 500 (a different transistor, or heat): A_f = 500/51 = **9.80**. A 50% change in A caused a **1%** change in A_f. That's the point.

## 3. What negative feedback buys — and costs

**Benefits:**
1. **Stable gain** — insensitive to transistor variations and temperature.
2. **Wider bandwidth** — gain × bandwidth stays roughly constant, so dividing gain by (1 + Aβ) multiplies bandwidth by about the same factor.
3. **Less distortion and noise** generated inside the loop — reduced by (1 + Aβ).
4. **Controlled input and output impedances** (next section).

**Cost:** lower gain — fixed by adding more stages, since gain is cheap and stability isn't.

## 4. Four topologies

Two questions: what's **sampled** at the output (voltage or current), and how it's **mixed** at the input (series or shunt/parallel)?

| Topology | Samples | Mixes | Input impedance | Output impedance | Stabilises | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Voltage-series** (series-shunt) | voltage | series | **↑** increases | **↓** decreases | voltage gain | op-amp non-inverting, emitter follower |
| **Voltage-shunt** (shunt-shunt) | voltage | shunt | ↓ | ↓ | transresistance | op-amp inverting |
| **Current-series** (series-series) | current | series | ↑ | ↑ | transconductance | CE stage with un-bypassed R_E |
| **Current-shunt** (shunt-series) | current | shunt | ↓ | ↑ | current gain | — |

Rule of thumb: **series mixing raises input impedance; shunt lowers it. Voltage sampling lowers output impedance; current sampling raises it** — each by the factor (1 + Aβ).

## 5. Stability

Too much feedback with too much phase shift around the loop can turn negative feedback into positive at some frequency, and the amplifier oscillates. Designers check **gain and phase margins** to make sure that never happens.

**Question.** What is negative feedback? Derive the gain with feedback and state its advantages.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Definition.** Negative feedback returns a fraction β of the output to the input in opposition to the input signal. *(Draw the block diagram: input → summing point → amplifier A → output, with β back to the summing point.)*

**Derivation.** Error signal `e = V_s − βV_o`; output `V_o = A e = A(V_s − βV_o)`. So `V_o(1 + Aβ) = A V_s`, giving **`A_f = A/(1 + Aβ)`**. If Aβ ≫ 1, A_f ≈ 1/β.

**Advantages.**
1. Gain becomes stable and predictable.
2. Bandwidth increases (gain–bandwidth product constant).
3. Non-linear distortion and internal noise reduce.
4. Input and output impedances can be raised or lowered as needed (series/shunt, voltage/current).

**Disadvantage.** Overall gain is reduced; excessive loop phase shift can cause oscillation.
