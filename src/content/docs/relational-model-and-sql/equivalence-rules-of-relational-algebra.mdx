The [previous article](../from-sql-to-relational-algebra-by-hand/) turned SQL into a correct tree, and
pointed out that the correct tree is rarely a fast one. It filters after joining, joins by forming a
product, and carries every column of every table to the top. An optimizer's first job is to turn that tree
into one that computes exactly the same result with far less work.

It does that with **equivalence rules**: pairs of expressions that always produce the same relation, so
one can replace the other anywhere. A rule is only as good as its side conditions. Moving a filter below a
join is safe when the filter mentions one side, and changes the answer when the join is an outer join. This
article lists the rules optimizers lean on most, tests each one on hundreds of random relations, breaks the
side conditions on purpose, and then watches DataFusion apply the rules one pass at a time.

## The question

Which rewrites of an algebra tree never change its result, and what exactly must be true for each one to
be safe?

## What "equivalent" means

Two expressions are **equivalent** if they produce the same relation for every possible database, not just
for today's data. That "every" is the whole difficulty. A rewrite that happens to give the same rows on the
test data can still be wrong. So the lab doesn't test rules on one example. It generates 500 random triples
of small relations, R(a, b), S(b, c) and T(a, b), with values drawn from a domain of four, so that joins
match and duplicates collide often, and checks each rule on all of them. A rule that holds 500 times out of
500 isn't proven by that, but a rule that fails once is disproven, and the lab prints the counterexample.

## Rules for selection

```text title="cargo run --release -p m01-relational --example equivalences"
σ cascade: σ p∧q (R) = σ p (σ q (R))                       held in 500 of 500
σ commutes: σ p (σ q (R)) = σ q (σ p (R))                  held in 500 of 500
σ pushdown: σ a>1 (R ⋈ S) = σ a>1 (R) ⋈ S                  held in 500 of 500
σ pushdown, other side: σ c>1 (R ⋈ S) = R ⋈ σ c>1 (S)      held in 500 of 500
σ over ∪: σ p (R ∪ T) = σ p (R) ∪ σ p (T)                  held in 500 of 500
σ over −: σ p (R − T) = σ p (R) − T                        held in 500 of 500
```

- **Cascade.** A selection with a conjunction equals a stack of selections, one per condition. It looks
  trivial, but it is what makes everything else possible: once `WHERE a AND b AND c` is three separate
  filters, each can travel on its own.
- **Commutativity.** Filters in a stack can swap places, so an optimizer can run the cheapest or most
  selective one first.
- **Pushdown through a join.** A selection above a join can move to the input that owns all of its columns.
  `a` belongs to R, so `σ a>1` can run on R before the join; `c` belongs to S, so `σ c>1` can run on S. A
  condition that mentions both sides, such as `a < c`, can't move to either side, and stays above the join or
  becomes part of the join condition.
- **Over set operations.** A selection distributes over union, and over difference it only needs to be
  applied to the left side: a row removed from R by the filter can't be in the result anyway.

## Rules for joins

```text title="cargo run --release -p m01-relational --example equivalences"
join commutes: R ⋈ S = S ⋈ R                               held in 500 of 500
join associates: (R ⋈ S) ⋈ T = R ⋈ (S ⋈ T)                 held in 500 of 500
```

Commutativity and associativity together mean that inner joins can be evaluated in any order. For a query
joining n tables, the number of possible orders grows faster than exponentially, and the cost of different
orders can differ by orders of magnitude. Choosing among them is the subject of
[Module 13](../../cost-based-optimization/); these two rules are what make the choice legal at all.

Two more rules connect joins with the operators beneath them. A selection directly above a product is a
join: σ θ (R × S) = R ⋈θ S. And a product is a join with no condition, so a comma in `FROM` plus an equality
in `WHERE` can always be planned as an equi-join.

## Rules for projection

```text title="cargo run --release -p m01-relational --example equivalences"
π cascade: π a (π a,b (R)) = π a (R)                       held in 500 of 500
π pushdown, keeping the join key: π a,c (R ⋈ S) = π a,c (π a,b (R) ⋈ S) held in 500 of 500
```

Projection pushdown removes columns early, so every operator above handles narrower rows. The side condition
is that the pushed projection must keep every column anything above it still needs: the output columns, and
the join keys. Here `b` is the join key between R and S, so it must survive the pushed projection even though
the query never outputs it.

## Breaking the side conditions

The same harness, with the conditions violated:

```text title="cargo run --release -p m01-relational --example equivalences"
π pushdown that drops the join key: π a,c (R ⋈ S) vs π a (R) × π c (S) held in 151 of 500
π over −: π a (R − T) vs π a (R) − π a (T)                 held in 238 of 500
π over ∪ (holds): π a (R ∪ T) = π a (R) ∪ π a (T)          held in 500 of 500
```

Dropping the join key before joining leaves nothing to join on: the result is a product, pairing every `a`
with every `c`. On the counterexample the lab found, the correct answer has one row and the rewrite has four.
The rule "held" 151 times only because many random relations were empty or tiny.

Projection doesn't distribute over difference, and the counterexample shows why. R contains (1, 2) and
(1, 3); T contains (1, 2). R − T keeps (1, 3), so `a = 1` is in π a (R − T). But π a (T) contains 1, so
π a (R) − π a (T) removes it. Projecting first merges rows that differ in the dropped column, and the
difference then removes too much. Union has no such problem, so π distributes over ∪ in all 500 trials.

## Watch an optimizer do it

DataFusion's `EXPLAIN VERBOSE` prints the plan after every optimizer pass. The lab runs it on a query
written the old way, with a comma join, and keeps only the passes that changed something:

```text title="cargo run --release -p m01-relational --example equivalences (trimmed)"
--- initial_logical_plan
Projection: c.name, o.amount
  Filter: c.id = o.cust_id AND c.region = Utf8("ASIA") AND o.amount > Int64(80)
    Cross Join:
      SubqueryAlias: c
        TableScan: customer
      SubqueryAlias: o
        TableScan: orders

--- logical_plan after eliminate_cross_join
Projection: c.name, o.amount
  Filter: c.region = Utf8View("ASIA") AND o.amount > Int32(80)
    Inner Join: c.id = o.cust_id
      ...

--- logical_plan after push_down_filter
Projection: c.name, o.amount
  Inner Join: c.id = o.cust_id
    SubqueryAlias: c
      Filter: customer.region = Utf8View("ASIA")
        TableScan: customer
    SubqueryAlias: o
      Filter: orders.amount > Int32(80)
        TableScan: orders

--- logical_plan after optimize_projections
Projection: c.name, o.amount
  Inner Join: c.id = o.cust_id
    SubqueryAlias: c
      Projection: customer.id, customer.name
        Filter: customer.region = Utf8View("ASIA")
          TableScan: customer projection=[id, name, region]
    SubqueryAlias: o
      Filter: orders.amount > Int32(80)
        TableScan: orders projection=[cust_id, amount]
```

Each pass is a rule from this article. `eliminate_cross_join` finds the equality in the filter and turns the
product into a join. `push_down_filter` splits the remaining conjunction and moves each piece to the side that
owns its column. `optimize_projections` prunes columns, keeping the join keys `id` and `cust_id`. (Two
earlier passes, `type_coercion` and `simplify_expressions`, also changed the text, by casting the literals.)

## Outer joins

Inner joins are forgiving. Outer joins are not, because the rows they add, padded with NULLs, are exactly the
rows a moved filter can wrongly keep or drop. The lab runs three versions of "customers and their orders over
80" with a left join:

```text title="cargo run --release -p m01-relational --example equivalences (condensed)"
> ... FROM customer c LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.cust_id = c.id WHERE o.amount > 80
| Asha | 90     |
| Asha | 250    |
| Dara | 610    |

> ... FROM customer c LEFT JOIN (SELECT * FROM orders WHERE amount > 80) o ON o.cust_id = c.id
7 rows: Asha 90, Asha 250, Bruno NULL, Chen NULL, Dara 610, Emeka NULL, Farah NULL

> ... FROM customer c LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.cust_id = c.id AND o.amount > 80
7 rows: the same as the second
```

The filter in `WHERE` runs after the left join and throws away the NULL-padded rows, because NULL > 80 is not
TRUE. Pushed into the orders input, it runs before the join, and the join then pads every customer without a
large order back in. Same filter, different answer. (The third form shows that a condition in `ON` behaves
like the pushed version: for an outer join, `ON` and `WHERE` are not interchangeable.)

DataFusion still ends up pushing the filter, legally. Its optimized plan for the first query is:

```text title="Optimized plan"
Projection: c.name, o.amount
  Inner Join: c.id = o.cust_id
    SubqueryAlias: c
      TableScan: customer projection=[id, name]
    SubqueryAlias: o
      Filter: orders.amount > Int32(80)
        TableScan: orders projection=[cust_id, amount]
```

The rule `eliminate_outer_join` "rewrites outer joins to inner joins when later filters reject the
NULL-extended rows". `o.amount > 80` is never TRUE for a padded row, so the left join's extra rows would all be
filtered anyway, and the query is really an inner join. Once it is one, the ordinary pushdown is valid. A
filter on the preserved side, such as `c.region = 'ASIA'`, can be pushed below a left join directly, and the
lab's plan shows exactly that. A filter that keeps NULLs, such as `o.amount IS NULL`, blocks both
rewrites: the lab's plan for it keeps the `Left Join` and the `Filter` above it.

<Aside type="note" title="The general theory">
Which outer join rewrites are valid is subtle enough to have a literature of its own. César Galindo-Legaria
and Arnon Rosenthal's "Outerjoin Simplification and Reordering for Query Optimization" (ACM TODS, 1997)
covers when outer joins can become inner joins and when they can be reordered.
</Aside>

## Costs and trade-offs

**Rules make plans legal, not good.** Equivalence rules say which trees compute the same result. Which of
them is cheapest depends on table sizes, selectivities and available indexes, which is the job of a cost
model. Pushing filters down and pruning columns is almost always a win, which is why rule-based passes
apply them unconditionally; join order is not, which is why it gets a cost model.

**Every rule needs its conditions checked.** The bugs in optimizers are rarely in the rules themselves; they
are in the conditions: a filter pushed through an outer join, a projection that drops a needed column, a
rewrite that assumes no NULLs or no duplicates. The [next article](../duplicate-sensitive-and-order-sensitive-operators/)
covers the last of those.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>Can σ c.region = 'ASIA' ∧ o.amount > c.credit_limit be pushed below the join of customer c and orders o?</summary>

Split it first. `c.region = 'ASIA'` mentions only customer, so it can go to the customer side.
`o.amount > c.credit_limit` mentions both, so it must stay above the join, or become part of the join
condition.
</details>

<details>
<summary>A query outputs only o.amount from customer c JOIN orders o ON c.id = o.cust_id. Which columns of customer must the pushed projection keep?</summary>

Just `c.id`, the join key. No customer column is output, but the join still needs `id` to match rows.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why may WHERE o.amount > 80 above a LEFT JOIN be rewritten as an INNER JOIN, while WHERE o.amount IS NULL may not?</summary>

The left join's extra rows have NULL in every orders column. `o.amount > 80` is never TRUE for them, so the
filter removes them all, and the result equals an inner join's. `o.amount IS NULL` is TRUE exactly for those
rows, so they are the result, and an inner join would lose them.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Give a reason π doesn't distribute over −, in one sentence.</summary>

Projecting first merges rows that differ only in a dropped column, so a value can be removed because of one
row of T even though another row of R with that value survives the difference.
</details>
